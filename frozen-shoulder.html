Frozen Shoulder:
Stages, Treatment & Recovery

It can feel like your shoulder has been welded shut — yet frozen shoulder almost always thaws. Here is what is happening inside the joint and how to speed the thaw.

What Is Frozen Shoulder?

Frozen shoulder, medically called adhesive capsulitis, is a condition in which the thin capsule that surrounds the shoulder joint becomes inflamed, thick and tight. The joint loses its normal “slack”, so movement becomes painful and then stiff. Typically both active movement (you lifting the arm) and passive movement (someone else lifting it) are restricted — this is the key feature that separates frozen shoulder from a rotator cuff tear, where passive movement is usually preserved.

It affects roughly 2–5% of adults, most often people between 40 and 60 years, and women slightly more than men. Having diabetes raises the risk several-fold (up to one in five people with diabetes may develop it at some point), and thyroid disease, prolonged immobilisation after surgery or injury, and Dupuytren’s contracture are also associated.

The Three Stages

1

Freezing (painful) stage — about 2–9 months

Pain is the main problem, often worse at night and when lying on the affected side. The shoulder gradually loses movement because moving it hurts and the capsule is inflamed.

2

Frozen (stiff) stage — about 4–12 months

Pain settles a little but stiffness is at its worst. Reaching overhead, behind the back (tucking in a shirt, fastening a bra) and rotating outward (combing hair) become very difficult.

3

Thawing stage — about 6 months to 2 years

Movement returns slowly. Most people regain good function, although a small amount of stiffness can remain in some.

The whole course commonly lasts 1–3 years if untreated. Treatment does not always shorten it dramatically, but it controls pain and protects movement while the shoulder recovers.

How Is It Diagnosed?

Diagnosis is mainly clinical: your doctor measures how far the shoulder moves with and without help and checks that the pattern fits. An X-ray is usually taken to rule out arthritis, a calcific deposit or a fracture/dislocation that can also stiffen the shoulder. Ultrasound or MRI is not routinely needed but may be used if a rotator cuff tear is suspected, if pain is out of proportion, or if the shoulder has not improved as expected. Blood sugar and thyroid tests are sensible in anyone who develops frozen shoulder without an obvious cause.

Treatment That Works

Most people improve without surgery. Treatment is matched to the stage:

Painful stage
  • Simple analgesics or anti-inflammatory tablets as advised by your doctor
  • Heat before and ice after gentle movement, whichever eases pain
  • A corticosteroid injection into the joint can reduce pain and improve movement over the next few weeks
  • Gentle, pain-limited movement — avoid forcing the stretch
Stiff & thawing stage
  • Guided physiotherapy: capsular stretching, pendulum and wall-slide exercises
  • Daily home stretching, short and frequent (5 minutes, 4–6 times a day)
  • Strengthening only after movement has improved
  • Hydrodilatation (capsular distension) in selected patients

Evidence shows that an early steroid injection combined with a regular stretching programme gives the best short-term relief, while physiotherapy alone is effective in many people. Intense, painful stretching is not better — it can inflame the capsule and set recovery back.

When Is a Procedure Needed?

If six months or more of well-delivered non-surgical treatment has not restored useful movement, or night pain remains disabling, two options are considered:

  • Manipulation under anaesthesia (MUA) — while you are asleep the surgeon gently moves the shoulder through its range to break the tight capsule. Risks include fracture or rotator-cuff injury, which is why it is done carefully and is less used in osteoporotic or older patients.
  • Arthroscopic capsular release — keyhole surgery divides the tight capsule under direct vision, followed by early physiotherapy. Results are reliable and complication rates are low, but it is reserved for people who have failed other treatments.

Both are followed by physiotherapy starting the same or next day; without it the stiffness can return.

What You Can Do at Home

  • Keep using the arm for light daily tasks — complete rest makes stiffness worse
  • Sleep with a pillow supporting the arm; avoid lying on the sore side
  • Do 3–4 short stretching sessions a day after a warm shower, when the tissue is looser
  • Pendulum swings, table slides, towel stretches behind the back and wall-walking are classic exercises — ask your physiotherapist to tailor them
  • If you have diabetes, keep blood sugar well controlled — it improves recovery and lowers the chance of the other shoulder being affected

About 1 in 5 people develop frozen shoulder in the opposite shoulder later, usually within a few years, but it tends to be milder if treated early.

When to See a Doctor Promptly

  • Shoulder pain after a fall or accident (possible fracture or dislocation)
  • Fever, redness, warmth or swelling of the joint (possible infection)
  • Pain with chest pressure, sweating or breathlessness — call emergency services; left shoulder pain can be cardiac
  • Neck pain spreading down the arm with numbness or weakness
  • Unexplained weight loss with persistent night pain

Common Questions

Will frozen shoulder go away on its own?

Usually yes, but slowly — often 1–3 years. Treatment mainly makes this period less painful and helps you keep more movement.

Can I exercise through the pain?

Gentle stretching that brings a mild pulling feeling is helpful. Sharp or lingering pain after stretching means you pushed too hard.

Is a steroid injection safe?

A limited number of injections given by a trained doctor are generally safe. Temporary blood-sugar rise in diabetics and short-lived flare of pain are the most common side effects.

Does it come back?

It rarely returns in the same shoulder, but the other shoulder can be affected in a minority of people.
