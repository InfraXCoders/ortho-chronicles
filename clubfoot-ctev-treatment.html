Clubfoot (CTEV):
Ponseti Treatment for Parents

Clubfoot (congenital talipes equinovarus) is one of the most treatable birth conditions — when treatment starts in the first weeks of life.

What Is Clubfoot?

In clubfoot (CTEV), one or both feet are turned inward and downward at birth, and the sole faces sideways or upward. It affects roughly 1 in 1,000 babies, boys more than girls, and about half of cases involve both feet. The cause is not fully known — it is not due to the baby’s position in the womb or something the mother did — but genes and some environmental factors contribute. Clubfoot is usually an isolated problem, though it can be associated with conditions such as spina bifida or arthrogryposis, so the baby is carefully examined. It is painless for the baby, but left untreated the foot stays stiff and deformed and the child walks on the outer edge of the foot.

The Ponseti Method — the Gold Standard

1

Gentle manipulation and casting (weekly)

Starting in the first 1–2 weeks of life, the doctor gently stretches the foot and applies a long-leg plaster cast, repeated every week for about 4–6 weeks. Each cast corrects one part of the deformity in a fixed sequence.

2

A small heel-cord release (tenotomy)

In about 80–90% of babies the Achilles tendon is tight and a minor procedure under local anaesthetic lengthens it. A final cast is worn for 3 weeks.

3

Bracing (foot abduction brace)

Full-time for about 3 months, then at night and nap time until the child is about 4–5 years old. The brace prevents relapse — wearing it is the single most important thing parents can do.

With this method, over 95% of feet are corrected without major surgery, and children walk, run and play sport.

What Parents Can Do

  • Start treatment as early as possible, ideally within the first 2–3 weeks, and keep every weekly appointment
  • Keep casts dry and check toes for colour, warmth and movement; call if toes are blue, cold or swollen, or the cast slips
  • Never skip the brace — most relapses happen when the brace is stopped early
  • Foot-friendly clothing and gentle handling; babies tolerate casts very well
  • Ask about free or subsidised programmes in India — many government hospitals run clubfoot clinics

Relapse and Other Treatments

If the foot starts to turn in again, the first step is repeat casting. For older children with relapse, a tendon transfer (anterior tibial tendon transfer) restores balance. Babies who arrive late, children with complex or syndromic clubfoot, or those not corrected by casting may need soft-tissue release surgery.

Long-term Outlook

Most treated children lead normal, active lives. The treated foot is often slightly smaller and the calf thinner than the other side, but function is usually excellent. Regular follow-up to age 5 and beyond catches relapse early.

Contact Your Doctor If

  • Toes become blue, pale, cold or very swollen
  • The cast slips, cracks or becomes wet and smelly
  • There is a fever or the baby is inconsolable
  • The brace causes skin sores
  • The foot starts to turn inward again after correction

Common Questions

Can clubfoot be seen before birth?

Often on a routine ultrasound at 18–20 weeks. Meeting a paediatric orthopaedic surgeon beforehand helps parents plan.

Does the baby feel pain with casts?

Babies fuss during the manipulation but are comfortable in the cast.

Is surgery always needed?

No — the Ponseti method avoids major surgery in most babies.

Will my next child have it?

The risk is higher (around 2–5%) if a sibling or parent has clubfoot, but still low.
