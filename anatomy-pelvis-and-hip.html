Pelvis & Hip Anatomy

Bones, muscles, ligaments, vessels and nerves — with origins, insertions, nerve supply, actions and the clinical points that matter, linked to a 3D model you can rotate.

Dr. Maninder SinghMS Orthopedics | Associate Professor, GMCH Amritsar
November 2026 12 min read

The hip is a deep ball-and-socket joint — stable, strong and load-bearing — set within the ring of the pelvis. The femoral head’s blood supply is delicate, which is why displaced femoral neck fractures and avascular necrosis are so consequential. The gluteal muscles hold the pelvis level in walking (a weak gluteus medius produces a Trendelenburg gait), the sciatic nerve passes beneath piriformis in the buttock, and the obturator and femoral nerves supply the thigh.

Bones

Pelvis (Hip Bones)

Os coxae · Bone · Pelvic Girdle

Each hip bone is formed by the fusion of ilium, ischium and pubis at the triradiate cartilage of the acetabulum (fusing around puberty). With the sacrum they form a closed ring that transfers the body weight from spine to lower limbs, protects the pelvic viscera, and — through its shape differences between sexes — determines the birth canal.

Articulations

  • Sacroiliac joints (posteriorly)
  • Pubic symphysis (anteriorly)
  • Hip joints (with femoral heads)

Muscle & ligament attachments

  • Gluteal group (outer ilium)
  • Iliacus (inner ilium)
  • Hamstrings (ischial tuberosity)
  • Adductor group (pubis)
  • Rectus femoris (anterior inferior iliac spine)
  • Abdominal wall muscles (iliac crest)

Key landmarks

  • Iliac crest — The palpable upper border of the ilium, at the L4 level. Its posterior part is the standard donor site for autologous bone graft.
  • Anterior superior iliac spine (ASIS) — Prominent anterior point — origin of sartorius and the inguinal ligament; the reference for measuring true leg length.
  • Posterior superior iliac spine (PSIS) — Marked by the dimples of Venus; overlies the S2 level and the sacroiliac joint.
  • Acetabulum — The cup receiving the femoral head, deepened by a fibrocartilaginous labrum. Fracture patterns here follow the two-column (anterior/posterior) concept.
  • Ischial tuberosity — The "sit bone" — origin of the hamstrings and the weight-bearing point when seated. Avulsion here is a classic adolescent sprinting injury.
  • Greater sciatic notch — Transmits the sciatic nerve, superior gluteal vessels and piriformis; a key surgical landmark in the posterior approach to the hip.
  • Pubic symphysis — A midline fibrocartilaginous joint; widening beyond 2.5 cm indicates an unstable "open-book" pelvic injury.
Unstable pelvic fracture. High-energy injury with haemodynamic instability: apply a pelvic binder at the level of the greater trochanters, resuscitate, and stabilise the ring — damage control before definitive fixation. Mortality is driven by bleeding from the posterior venous plexus.
Bone graft harvest. The anterior and posterior iliac crests remain the gold-standard autograft sources for non-union surgery, supplying osteogenic cells, growth factors and structural scaffold.

Muscles

Pelvic Floor (Levator Ani) (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Musculi diaphragmatis pelvis · Muscle · Musculoskeletal

The muscular hammock slung across the pelvic outlet — pubococcygeus, iliococcygeus and puborectalis, with coccygeus behind. It supports the pelvic viscera, maintains continence and resists rises in intra-abdominal pressure.

Origin
Pubis, tendinous arch of the obturator fascia, ischial spine
Insertion
Perineal body, anococcygeal ligament, coccyx
Nerve
Nerve to levator ani (S3–S5) and branches of the pudendal nerve
Action
Supports pelvic viscera; continence; raises the pelvic floor
Blood supply
Inferior gluteal, inferior vesical and pudendal branches of the internal iliac artery.
Venous drainage
To the internal iliac vein.
Lymphatic drainage
Internal iliac and sacral nodes.
Clinical relevance. Its S2–S4 innervation is exactly the territory lost in cauda equina syndrome, which is why sphincter tone and saddle sensation are the decisive examination findings. Obstetric injury here is the leading cause of pelvic organ prolapse and stress incontinence, and the floor works as a functional unit with transversus abdominis and multifidus in core stability.

Iliopsoas (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Musculus iliopsoas · Muscle · Musculoskeletal

Psoas major (from the lumbar vertebrae) joins iliacus (from the iliac fossa) to form the iliopsoas, the most powerful hip flexor, inserting on the lesser trochanter.

Origin
Psoas — bodies and transverse processes of T12–L5; Iliacus — iliac fossa
Insertion
Lesser trochanter of the femur
Nerve
Psoas — L1–L3 ventral rami; Iliacus — femoral nerve (L2, L3)
Action
Hip flexion; with the femur fixed, flexes the trunk; stabilises the lumbar spine
Blood supply
Lumbar and iliolumbar arteries; iliacus also by the iliac branch of the iliolumbar and the deep circumflex iliac.
Venous drainage
To the lumbar and iliac veins.
Lymphatic drainage
Common iliac and lumbar (para-aortic) nodes.
Clinical relevance. A psoas abscess (from spinal TB or Crohn disease) causes a flexed, externally rotated hip and a positive psoas stretch test. Snapping of the iliopsoas tendon over the pelvic brim is a common cause of anterior hip pain, and the tendon is a recognised source of groin pain after hip replacement.

Deep Hip Rotators (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Musculi rotatores coxae · Muscle · Musculoskeletal

Six short external rotators running from the sacrum and ischium to the greater trochanter — piriformis, obturator internus and externus, the superior and inferior gemelli, and quadratus femoris. They are the rotator cuff of the hip: fine stabilisers rather than prime movers.

Origin
Anterior sacrum (piriformis), obturator membrane and ischium
Insertion
Greater trochanter and trochanteric fossa
Nerve
Nerve to obturator internus, nerve to quadratus femoris, sacral plexus branches (L5–S2)
Action
External rotation of the extended hip; abduction when the hip is flexed; dynamic stabilisation of the femoral head
Blood supply
Inferior gluteal, internal pudendal and medial circumflex femoral arteries.
Venous drainage
To the internal iliac vein.
Lymphatic drainage
Internal iliac (deep gluteal) nodes.
Clinical relevance. The sciatic nerve emerges directly beneath piriformis — in about 15% of people it pierces the muscle, the anatomical basis of piriformis syndrome. These tendons are divided in the posterior approach to the hip and must be repaired: failure to do so is a recognised contributor to posterior dislocation after hip replacement.

Gluteus Maximus (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Musculus gluteus maximus · Muscle · Musculoskeletal

The bulkiest muscle in the body, and the principal extensor of the hip. It works hardest in powerful actions — rising from a chair, climbing stairs, sprinting — rather than in level walking.

Origin
Posterior ilium, sacrum, coccyx, sacrotuberous ligament
Insertion
Iliotibial tract and gluteal tuberosity of the femur
Nerve
Inferior gluteal nerve (L5, S1, S2)
Action
Hip extension and lateral rotation; through the IT band it stabilises the extended knee
Blood supply
Inferior gluteal artery (chief) and the superior gluteal artery.
Venous drainage
Gluteal veins to the internal iliac vein.
Lymphatic drainage
Deep gluteal to internal iliac nodes; superficial part to superficial inguinal.
Clinical relevance. The upper outer quadrant of the buttock is the safe zone for intramuscular injection — injecting elsewhere risks the sciatic nerve, a classic and avoidable cause of foot drop.

Gluteus Medius (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Musculus gluteus medius · Muscle · Musculoskeletal

The key abductor of the hip. Its real job is not lifting the leg sideways but stabilising the pelvis in single-leg stance — every step you take depends on it.

Origin
Outer surface of the ilium between the anterior and posterior gluteal lines
Insertion
Lateral facet of the greater trochanter
Nerve
Superior gluteal nerve (L4, L5, S1)
Action
Hip abduction; anterior fibres medially rotate; stabilises the pelvis in stance
Blood supply
Superior gluteal artery.
Venous drainage
Superior gluteal vein to the internal iliac vein.
Lymphatic drainage
Deep gluteal to internal iliac nodes.
Clinical relevance. Weakness (or superior gluteal nerve injury) produces a positive Trendelenburg sign — the pelvis drops on the *unsupported* side — and the resulting lurching gait. Protecting this muscle and its nerve is central to the surgical approach chosen for hip replacement.

Tensor Fasciae Latae & Iliotibial Band (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

M. tensor fasciae latae / Tractus iliotibialis · Muscle · Musculoskeletal

A small muscle at the front of the hip continuing into the iliotibial band — a thick fascial strap running down the lateral thigh to Gerdy tubercle on the tibia, bracing the extended knee.

Origin
Anterior iliac crest and ASIS
Insertion
Iliotibial tract → Gerdy tubercle of the tibia
Nerve
Superior gluteal nerve (L4, L5, S1)
Action
Hip flexion, abduction and internal rotation; lateral knee stabilisation
Blood supply
Ascending branch of the lateral circumflex femoral and the superior gluteal arteries.
Venous drainage
Corresponding venae comitantes.
Lymphatic drainage
Superficial inguinal and deep gluteal nodes.
Clinical relevance. Iliotibial band syndrome causes lateral knee pain in runners and cyclists as the band moves over the lateral femoral epicondyle. A tight band also contributes to lateral patellar tracking and anterior knee pain.

Adductor Group (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Musculi adductores · Muscle · Musculoskeletal

Adductor longus, brevis and magnus with gracilis and pectineus, running from the pubis to the linea aspera. Adductor magnus also has a hamstring part reaching the adductor tubercle.

Origin
Body and inferior ramus of the pubis; ischial tuberosity (magnus, hamstring part)
Insertion
Linea aspera of the femur; adductor tubercle; medial tibia (gracilis)
Nerve
Obturator nerve (L2–L4); tibial division of sciatic to the hamstring part of magnus
Action
Hip adduction; assist flexion and rotation; stabilise the pelvis in stance
Blood supply
Profunda femoris (perforating and medial circumflex femoral) and obturator arteries.
Venous drainage
To the femoral and obturator veins.
Lymphatic drainage
Deep inguinal to external iliac nodes.
Clinical relevance. Adductor longus strain ("groin strain") is common in football and hockey. The adductor hiatus in adductor magnus transmits the femoral vessels to the popliteal fossa — a site where the artery is tethered and vulnerable.

Tendons & ligaments

Iliotibial Tract (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Tractus iliotibialis · Tendon · Connective Tissue

A thick longitudinal thickening of the fascia lata running down the lateral thigh from the iliac crest to Gerdy tubercle on the tibia, receiving the insertions of tensor fasciae latae and much of gluteus maximus.

Origin
Iliac crest, via tensor fasciae latae and gluteus maximus
Insertion
Gerdy tubercle on the anterolateral tibia
Function
Braces the extended knee; lateral stabiliser of hip and knee
Structure
Dense parallel collagen — a fascial band, not a true tendon
Clinical relevance. Iliotibial band syndrome causes lateral knee pain in runners and cyclists as the band moves over the lateral femoral epicondyle at about 30° of flexion. It is also the lateral landmark for the modified Gerdy approach, and a tight band contributes to lateral patellar maltracking.

Arteries

Internal Iliac Artery (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Arteria iliaca interna · Artery · Cardiovascular

The artery of the pelvis, arising from the common iliac bifurcation at the sacroiliac joint. It supplies the pelvic viscera, walls, perineum and gluteal region through anterior and posterior divisions.

Origin
Common iliac artery, at the level of the sacroiliac joint
Branches
Superior and inferior gluteal, obturator, internal pudendal, visceral branches
Supplies
Pelvic organs, gluteal muscles, perineum, hip joint (via obturator)
Relations
Lies anterior to the sacroiliac joint and lumbosacral trunk
Clinical relevance. Its branches — especially the superior gluteal artery — are the main source of catastrophic haemorrhage in unstable pelvic fractures. This is why a pelvic binder plus angiographic embolisation or pelvic packing is the resuscitation pathway in an unstable pelvis.

Profunda Femoris & Circumflex Femoral Arteries (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

A. profunda femoris · Artery · Cardiovascular

The deep artery of the thigh, arising from the femoral artery in the femoral triangle. Its medial circumflex femoral branch winds posteriorly around the femoral neck and supplies the great majority of the femoral head's blood.

Origin
Femoral artery, about 4 cm below the inguinal ligament
Branches
Medial and lateral circumflex femoral arteries; four perforating arteries
Supplies
Femoral head and neck, thigh musculature
Course
Medial circumflex passes posteriorly between pectineus and iliopsoas to the trochanteric fossa
Clinical relevance. The medial circumflex femoral artery is the artery of the femoral head — this is why displaced intracapsular neck fractures in the elderly are replaced rather than fixed, and why the posterior approach and femoral neck osteotomy must respect it. Its retinacular branches run in the capsule along the neck.

Nerves

Sciatic Nerve (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Nervus ischiadicus · Nerve · Peripheral Nervous System

The largest nerve in the body — as thick as a thumb at its origin. Formed from the L4–S3 roots of the sacral plexus, it leaves the pelvis through the greater sciatic foramen below piriformis and descends through the posterior thigh, dividing above the knee.

Roots
L4, L5, S1, S2, S3
Course
Greater sciatic foramen (below piriformis) → midway between the ischial tuberosity and greater trochanter → posterior thigh
Divisions
Tibial and common peroneal nerves, usually at the upper popliteal fossa
Supplies
All hamstrings, and everything below the knee via its two divisions
Clinical relevance. True sciatica is most often caused by lumbar disc herniation at L4-L5 or L5-S1 rather than by the nerve trunk itself. The nerve is at risk in posterior hip dislocation, acetabular fracture and hip surgery — and in badly sited gluteal injections, which is why only the upper outer quadrant is used.

Superior Gluteal Nerve (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Nervus gluteus superior · Nerve · Peripheral Nervous System

Leaves the pelvis through the greater sciatic foramen *above* piriformis, running between gluteus medius and minimus to supply the hip abductors and tensor fasciae latae.

Roots
L4, L5, S1
Course
Greater sciatic foramen above piriformis, then between gluteus medius and minimus
Motor
Gluteus medius, gluteus minimus, tensor fasciae latae
Sensory
None
Clinical relevance. Injury abolishes hip abduction and produces a positive Trendelenburg sign with a lurching gait. It runs roughly 3–5 cm above the greater trochanter, which limits how far proximally the lateral (Hardinge) approach to the hip can be extended — the classic cause of iatrogenic abductor weakness after hip replacement.

Inferior Gluteal Nerve (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Nervus gluteus inferior · Nerve · Peripheral Nervous System

Leaves the pelvis through the greater sciatic foramen *below* piriformis — the counterpart of the superior gluteal nerve — and supplies gluteus maximus alone.

Roots
L5, S1, S2
Course
Greater sciatic foramen, inferior to piriformis
Motor
Gluteus maximus only
Sensory
None
Clinical relevance. Injury makes it hard to rise from a chair, climb stairs or run, though level walking is largely preserved because gluteus maximus is not essential to it. It lies close to the sciatic nerve below piriformis, so both are threatened together in posterior hip approaches and in badly placed gluteal injections.

Obturator Nerve (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Nervus obturatorius · Nerve · Peripheral Nervous System

The nerve of the medial thigh, arising from the lumbar plexus, running along the lateral pelvic wall and entering the thigh through the obturator foramen to supply the adductor group.

Roots
L2, L3, L4 (anterior divisions)
Course
Medial border of psoas, along the lateral pelvic wall, through the obturator canal
Motor
Adductor longus, brevis and magnus, gracilis, obturator externus
Sensory
Skin of the medial thigh
Clinical relevance. It is at risk in acetabular fracture surgery and pelvic procedures. It also explains referred pain: hip pathology commonly refers to the medial thigh and knee via this nerve, which is why a child with knee pain must always have the hip examined.

Pudendal Nerve (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Nervus pudendus · Nerve · Peripheral Nervous System

The main nerve of the perineum, from S2–S4. It leaves the pelvis through the greater sciatic foramen, hooks around the sacrospinous ligament and re-enters through the lesser sciatic foramen into the pudendal canal.

Roots
S2, S3, S4 — "S2, 3, 4 keeps the pelvic floor off the floor"
Course
Greater sciatic foramen → around the ischial spine → lesser sciatic foramen → pudendal (Alcock) canal
Motor
External anal and urethral sphincters, pelvic floor muscles
Sensory
Perineum and external genitalia
Clinical relevance. It carries the sacral sphincter control that defines cauda equina syndrome — saddle anaesthesia and sphincter failure are its territory. It is also compressed against the ischial spine by prolonged cycling, and is at risk from traction-table perineal posts during femoral nailing.

Lateral Femoral Cutaneous Nerve (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Nervus cutaneus femoris lateralis · Nerve · Peripheral Nervous System

A purely sensory nerve from the lumbar plexus that crosses the iliacus and passes under the inguinal ligament just medial to the anterior superior iliac spine to supply the lateral thigh.

Roots
L2, L3
Course
Across iliacus, under the inguinal ligament medial to the ASIS
Sensory
Anterolateral thigh skin
Motor
None
Clinical relevance. Compression here causes meralgia paraesthetica — burning, numbness and tingling over the lateral thigh, provoked by tight belts, obesity, pregnancy or prone positioning. It is also the nerve most often injured during anterior iliac crest bone graft harvest and the direct anterior approach to the hip.

Iliohypogastric & Ilioinguinal Nerves (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

N. iliohypogastricus et ilioinguinalis · Nerve · Peripheral Nervous System

The first branches of the lumbar plexus (L1), running around the abdominal wall between the internal oblique and transversus to supply the lower abdominal muscles and the skin above the pubis and groin.

Roots
L1 (with a contribution from T12)
Course
Across quadratus lumborum, then between transversus abdominis and internal oblique
Motor
Lower fibres of internal oblique and transversus abdominis
Sensory
Suprapubic skin (iliohypogastric); groin, scrotum or labium (ilioinguinal)
Clinical relevance. These are the nerves injured in appendicectomy, hernia repair and Pfannenstiel incisions, producing chronic neuropathic groin pain and, if the motor branches go, a weak abdominal wall that can bulge like a hernia. They are also the target of the transversus abdominis plane (TAP) block.

Lymphatics

Inguinal Lymph Nodes (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Nodi lymphoidei inguinales · Lymphatics · Immune System

Superficial and deep groups in the femoral triangle. The superficial nodes lie along the inguinal ligament and the great saphenous vein; the deep nodes lie medial to the femoral vein, including the node of Cloquet in the femoral canal.

Superficial
Horizontal group along the inguinal ligament; vertical group along the great saphenous vein
Deep
Medial to the femoral vein, including the node of Cloquet
Afferents
Lower limb, lower abdominal wall, perineum, external genitalia, anal canal below the pectinate line
Efferents
External iliac nodes → para-aortic nodes → thoracic duct
Clinical relevance. Enlarged inguinal nodes are common and often reactive to trivial foot or leg infection — always examine the whole limb and perineum before escalating. Groin dissection carries a high risk of lymphoedema and wound complications.

Para-aortic & Iliac Nodes (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Nodi lymphoidei lumbales et iliaci · Lymphatics · Immune System

The nodal chains lying alongside the aorta and iliac vessels in the retroperitoneum. They receive lymph from the pelvis, gonads, kidneys and lower limbs and funnel it into the cisterna chyli.

Location
Alongside the abdominal aorta and IVC (para-aortic); along the common, external and internal iliac vessels
Afferents
Pelvic viscera, gonads (directly, via their embryological descent), kidneys, lower limbs via iliac chain
Efferents
Lumbar lymph trunks → cisterna chyli → thoracic duct
Clinical relevance. Testicular tumours metastasise to the para-aortic nodes — not the inguinal nodes — because the testis develops in the abdomen and drags its lymphatics with it. This is why a testicular mass warrants abdominal imaging rather than a groin examination alone.

Lumbar Lymph Trunks (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Trunci lumbales · Lymphatics · Immune System

Paired trunks ascending alongside the aorta and inferior vena cava, carrying lymph from the lower limbs, pelvis, kidneys and gonads up to the cisterna chyli.

Formation
Efferents of the lateral aortic (para-aortic) node chains
Afferents
Lower limbs, pelvic viscera, kidneys, adrenals, gonads, deep abdominal wall
Termination
Cisterna chyli at L1–L2
Relations
Run on either side of the abdominal aorta, behind the peritoneum
Clinical relevance. Retroperitoneal lymph node dissection for testicular tumours follows precisely these chains, and injuring them causes chylous ascites. Their route explains why a testicular mass metastasises to the para-aortic nodes rather than the groin.
