Knee Osteoarthritis:
What Works Without Surgery

Knee arthritis does not automatically mean knee replacement. Most people can stay active for years with the right combination of exercise, weight control and targeted treatment.

Understanding Knee Osteoarthritis

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a gradual wearing and remodelling of the joint: the smooth cartilage that cushions the bone ends thins, the bone beneath thickens, bony spurs form, and the joint lining becomes irritated. It is the most common joint condition worldwide and the knee is among the joints most affected, especially in people over 50. Pain with walking, stairs and squatting, morning stiffness under 30 minutes, creaking, swelling after activity and gradual loss of bending are typical.

In India the picture is shaped by the way we live: floor sitting, squatting, Indian-style toilets and prayer positions load the knee in deep flexion, and rising obesity and low vitamin D add to the burden. Past knee injury, malalignment (bow-legs or knock-knees) and inflammatory arthritis are other contributors.

How It Is Diagnosed and Graded

Diagnosis is clinical, supported by a weight-bearing X-ray that shows joint-space narrowing, spurs and alignment. The Kellgren–Lawrence grading (1 to 4) is often quoted, but the X-ray is only part of the story: some people with severe-looking X-rays have little pain, while others with early changes are quite uncomfortable. MRI is rarely required for typical arthritis; it is useful when a meniscal tear, avascular necrosis or other cause is suspected. Blood tests help exclude rheumatoid arthritis, gout or infection when swelling is marked.

The Core of Treatment

1

Exercise — the strongest evidence

Quadriceps and hip-strengthening, low-impact aerobic exercise (cycling, swimming, brisk walking) and balance work reduce pain and improve function, with effects comparable to some medicines. Aim for 30 minutes on most days; start gently.

2

Weight loss

Each kilogram lost removes about 3–4 kg of load from the knee with every step. Losing 5–10% of body weight can noticeably reduce pain.

3

Activity modification

Use a commode or raised seat, avoid deep squatting and long stair climbing when painful, and use a walking stick in the opposite hand to share load.

4

Heat, supports and footwear

Warm packs for stiffness, cold packs after flare-ups. A knee sleeve can improve confidence; cushioned shoes help.

Medicines and Injections

Always use medicines under medical guidance — especially if you have kidney, stomach, heart or blood-pressure problems.

Tablets & gels
  • Paracetamol for mild pain (modest effect)
  • Topical NSAID gel is a safe first anti-inflammatory choice for the knee
  • Oral NSAIDs for short courses, with stomach protection if needed
  • Glucosamine/chondroitin: evidence of benefit is weak and inconsistent
Injections
  • Corticosteroid: quick relief of a flare for weeks; repeated frequent use may accelerate cartilage loss
  • Hyaluronic acid: benefit modest and variable; some guidelines advise against routine use
  • PRP: promising for early-to-moderate OA, but quality, preparation and cost vary
  • None of these regrow cartilage

When Is Surgery the Right Choice?

Surgery is considered when pain and stiffness persist despite 3–6 months of good non-surgical care and they limit walking, sleep or daily life. Options depend on age, alignment and severity:

  • Osteotomy (re-aligning the bone) — for younger, active patients with early arthritis in one compartment
  • Partial knee replacement — only one compartment is damaged
  • Total knee replacement — the most reliable treatment for advanced arthritis; over 90% of implants last 15–20 years
  • Arthroscopic “wash-out” is not recommended for degenerative arthritis; trials show no lasting benefit

Seek Care Early If

  • A hot, red, swollen knee with fever (possible infection or gout)
  • A knee that locks, gives way or follows an injury
  • Severe night pain or a sudden worsening, or pain with unexplained weight loss
  • Calf swelling and pain after a long period of immobility (possible clot)

Common Questions

Can knee arthritis be cured?

There is no cure that regrows cartilage, but symptoms can be well controlled for years with exercise, weight control and targeted treatment.

Is walking bad for arthritic knees?

No. Regular walking and low-impact exercise are good for the joint; avoid pushing through sharp pain.

Should I stop squatting and sitting cross-legged?

You don’t have to give it up entirely, but when the knee is painful limit deep flexion and use a chair or commode.

Do injections delay a knee replacement?

Steroid injections give short-term relief; none have been proven to prevent progression of arthritis.
