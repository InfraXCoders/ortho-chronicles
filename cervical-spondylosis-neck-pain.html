Cervical Spondylosis:
Neck Pain Explained

Hours on laptops and phones are straining our necks earlier than ever. Understand what is wear-and-tear, what is worrying, and what you can do today.

What Is Cervical Spondylosis?

Cervical spondylosis is age-related wear of the neck (cervical) spine: the discs lose height and hydration, small bony spurs (osteophytes) form and the facet joints thicken. By the age of 50, most adults show some of these changes on X-ray or MRI, and many have no symptoms. When symptoms occur, they include neck pain and stiffness, headaches from the back of the head, shoulder-blade pain and a grinding feeling on turning.

Modern habits add to the strain: long hours with the head bent forward over a phone or laptop (“text neck”), poor workstation set-up, carrying weight on one shoulder, sleeping on a high pillow, and stress that keeps the neck muscles tight. Previous neck injury and a family history also play a role.

Three Patterns of Problem

Neck pain only (axial)
  • Muscle spasm and joint irritation
  • No arm symptoms
  • Responds to exercise, posture correction and short-term medication
Radiculopathy (pinched nerve)
  • Pain, tingling or weakness spreading into the shoulder, arm or fingers
  • Often worse on looking up and turning to the affected side
  • Usually improves in 6–12 weeks with non-surgical care
Myelopathy (spinal cord compression)
  • Clumsy hands, trouble with buttons or handwriting
  • Unsteady, “heavy” walking and tripping
  • Needs urgent specialist assessment — often surgical

Tests

Examination checks neck movement, reflexes, strength and sensation. X-rays (including flexion/extension views) show spurs, disc height and alignment. MRI is the best test to see discs, the cord and nerve roots and is advised for arm symptoms that persist, any sign of cord compression, or before treatment such as injection or surgery. Nerve conduction tests help separate a pinched neck nerve from carpal tunnel or ulnar nerve problems.

Treatment and Self-Care

1

Stay active, avoid a rigid collar

Gentle movement heals faster than immobilisation. A soft collar is only for a few days in severe pain.

2

Posture and workstation

Set the screen at eye level, keep elbows at 90°, feet flat and forearms supported. Hold the phone up rather than looking down. Take a short break every 30 minutes.

3

Exercises

Chin tucks, gentle neck rotations and side-bends, shoulder-blade squeezes and upper-back strengthening, done daily within comfort. A physiotherapist can individualise the programme and may add traction or manual therapy.

4

Medication

Paracetamol, short courses of anti-inflammatory tablets, muscle relaxants for spasm and nerve-pain medicines in radiculopathy — as prescribed.

5

Sleep set-up

Use a thin pillow that keeps the neck in line with the spine; avoid sleeping on your stomach.

When Is Surgery Considered?

  • Signs of myelopathy (cord compression) — usually surgery is advised to prevent further deterioration
  • Progressive arm weakness
  • Severe arm pain that has not improved after about 6–12 weeks of proper conservative treatment
  • Common operations: anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF), cervical disc replacement in selected patients, or posterior decompression (laminoplasty/laminectomy)

Urgent Warning Signs

  • Loss of balance, difficulty walking or clumsy hands
  • Weakness or numbness in both arms or legs
  • Bladder or bowel control problems
  • Neck pain with fever, severe headache or neck stiffness that prevents touching chin to chest (possible meningitis)
  • Neck pain after a fall or road accident — do not move the neck; get emergency care
  • Neck, jaw or arm pain with chest pressure and sweating — possible heart attack

Common Questions

Does cracking my neck damage it?

Occasional painless cracking is harmless. Forceful manipulation by untrained persons can injure arteries or nerves and should be avoided.

Is cervical spondylosis permanent?

The age-related change remains, but symptoms usually settle and can be controlled with exercise and posture care.

Which pillow is best?

One that keeps your head level with your spine — for most people a thin pillow or a small neck-supporting roll.

Can mobile use cause spondylosis?

It does not cause it directly, but sustained forward-head posture overloads neck muscles and joints and aggravates symptoms.
