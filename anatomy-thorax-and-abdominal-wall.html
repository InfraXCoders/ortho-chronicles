Chest Wall, Abdominal Wall & Great Vessels Anatomy

Bones, muscles, ligaments, vessels and nerves — with origins, insertions, nerve supply, actions and the clinical points that matter, linked to a 3D model you can rotate.

Dr. Maninder SinghMS Orthopedics | Associate Professor, GMCH Amritsar
November 2026 8 min read

The thoracic cage protects the heart and lungs and provides the origin of many shoulder and trunk muscles. The abdominal wall muscles stabilise the trunk and, with the spine, form the core. The aorta and vena cava run along the front of the spine, so anterior spinal approaches require careful vascular exposure, and the thoracic duct carries lymph from most of the body into the left venous angle.

Bones

Thoracic Cage (Ribs & Sternum)

Cavea thoracis · Bone · Thoracic Cage

Twelve pairs of ribs, the sternum and the thoracic vertebrae form a mobile bony cage that protects the heart and lungs and drives ventilation. Ribs 1–7 are "true" ribs joining the sternum by their own costal cartilage; 8–10 are "false" ribs sharing a cartilage; 11 and 12 are "floating" ribs with no anterior attachment. Rib elevation in inspiration works by "bucket-handle" and "pump-handle" mechanics.

Articulations

  • Costovertebral & costotransverse joints (posteriorly)
  • Sternocostal joints (anteriorly)
  • Sternoclavicular joint

Muscle & ligament attachments

  • Intercostal muscles
  • Diaphragm (costal margin)
  • Pectoralis major/minor, serratus anterior
  • Rectus abdominis (costal cartilages 5–7)

Key landmarks

  • Sternal angle (of Louis) — Palpable ridge where manubrium meets body — marks the 2nd costal cartilage, the T4/T5 disc, the aortic arch and the tracheal bifurcation. The single most useful landmark in the chest.
  • Suprasternal notch — The jugular notch at the top of the manubrium — surface marker for the trachea and a reference for central line placement.
  • Xiphoid process — Cartilaginous tip of the sternum; the landmark for hand position in chest compressions and for the subxiphoid pericardial window.
  • Costal margin — The lower border of the cage formed by cartilages 7–10 — the surface boundary between thorax and abdomen.
  • Safe triangle (axilla) — Bordered by latissimus dorsi, pectoralis major and the 5th intercostal space — the recommended site for chest drain insertion.
  • First rib — Short, flat and deeply placed; grooves for the subclavian vessels. A fractured first rib signals very high-energy trauma.
Flail chest. Three or more consecutive ribs fractured in two places produce a paradoxically moving segment. The threat is the underlying pulmonary contusion — treatment centres on analgesia, physiotherapy and ventilatory support.
Neurovascular bundle. Each intercostal vein, artery and nerve runs in the groove at the *lower* border of the rib — so needles and drains are always passed over the *upper* border of the rib below to avoid them.

Muscles

Transversus Thoracis (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Musculus transversus thoracis · Muscle · Musculoskeletal

A thin fan on the inner surface of the anterior chest wall, radiating from the back of the sternum to the costal cartilages, deep to the internal thoracic vessels.

Origin
Posterior surface of the lower sternum and xiphoid
Insertion
Inner surfaces of costal cartilages 2–6
Nerve
Intercostal nerves
Action
Depresses the ribs in forced expiration
Blood supply
Internal thoracic (internal mammary) artery.
Venous drainage
Internal thoracic veins.
Lymphatic drainage
Parasternal (internal thoracic) nodes.
Clinical relevance. It forms the plane immediately deep to the internal thoracic artery, and the transversus thoracis plane block that targets that plane is now used for analgesia after sternotomy and in anterior rib fractures.

Anterior Abdominal Wall (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Musculi abdominis · Muscle · Musculoskeletal

The layered muscular wall of the abdomen: rectus abdominis running vertically from ribs to pubis inside the rectus sheath, wrapped by external oblique, internal oblique and transversus abdominis. Together they flex and rotate the trunk and generate the intra-abdominal pressure that braces the lumbar spine.

Origin
Lower ribs and costal cartilages, thoracolumbar fascia, iliac crest, inguinal ligament
Insertion
Linea alba, pubic crest and symphysis
Nerve
Thoracoabdominal nerves (T7–T12), iliohypogastric and ilioinguinal (L1)
Action
Trunk flexion and rotation; raises intra-abdominal pressure; supports the viscera
Blood supply
Superior epigastric (from the internal thoracic) and inferior epigastric (from the external iliac) arteries.
Venous drainage
Superior and inferior epigastric veins.
Lymphatic drainage
Above the umbilicus to parasternal nodes; below to superficial inguinal.
Clinical relevance. Core strength genuinely matters in low back pain, but training the deep layer — transversus abdominis with multifidus — outperforms sit-ups alone. The aponeuroses of these muscles form the rectus sheath and inguinal canal, the anatomy behind hernia repair, and the layers are the target of the TAP block. Diastasis recti after pregnancy responds to graded core rehabilitation.

External Oblique (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Musculus obliquus externus abdominis · Muscle · Musculoskeletal

The largest and most superficial of the flat abdominal muscles, its fibres running downward and forward — "hands in pockets" — from the lower ribs to the iliac crest and linea alba.

Origin
External surfaces of ribs 5–12
Insertion
Iliac crest, pubic tubercle and linea alba via its aponeurosis
Nerve
Thoracoabdominal nerves (T7–T11), subcostal and iliohypogastric nerves
Action
Compresses the abdomen; flexes and contralaterally rotates the trunk
Blood supply
Lower posterior intercostal, subcostal and deep circumflex iliac arteries.
Venous drainage
Corresponding segmental veins.
Lymphatic drainage
Above the umbilicus to axillary (pectoral); below to superficial inguinal nodes.
Clinical relevance. Its aponeurosis forms the inguinal ligament and the anterior wall of the inguinal canal — the anatomy behind inguinal hernia repair. Together with the other abdominal muscles it generates the intra-abdominal pressure that unloads the lumbar spine, which is why core training genuinely helps chronic low back pain.

Arteries

Aorta (Arch, Thoracic & Abdominal)

Aorta · Artery · Cardiovascular

The great arterial trunk of the body. It leaves the left ventricle, arches over the left main bronchus (at the sternal angle / T4), descends through the thorax and abdomen, and bifurcates into the common iliac arteries at the level of L4.

Origin
Left ventricle of the heart
Branches
Coronary arteries; brachiocephalic trunk, left common carotid and left subclavian from the arch; intercostals; coeliac, superior and inferior mesenteric, renal arteries
Termination
Bifurcates into right and left common iliac arteries at L4
Surface
The arch lies at the level of the sternal angle (T4/T5)
Clinical relevance. The aortic bifurcation at L4 sits at the level of the iliac crests — a critical relationship in anterior lumbar spine surgery. Traumatic aortic injury classically occurs at the isthmus just distal to the left subclavian, where the relatively mobile arch meets the fixed descending aorta.

Veins

Superior & Inferior Vena Cava

Vena cava superior et inferior · Vein · Cardiovascular

The two great venous trunks returning all systemic blood to the right atrium. The SVC drains the head, neck and upper limbs; the IVC — the largest vein in the body — drains everything below the diaphragm and ascends to the right of the vertebral bodies.

Formation
SVC — from the right and left brachiocephalic veins; IVC — from the common iliac veins at L5
Course
IVC ascends to the right of the aorta, piercing the diaphragm at T8
Termination
Both drain into the right atrium
Tributaries
Azygos system, hepatic, renal, gonadal and lumbar veins
Clinical relevance. IVC filters may be considered when there is a proximal DVT and anticoagulation is contraindicated — a scenario that arises regularly in major orthopaedic trauma. SVC obstruction causes facial swelling and distended neck veins.

Azygos & Hemiazygos System

Vena azygos · Vein · Cardiovascular

A longitudinal venous chain on the posterior thoracic wall draining the intercostal spaces and posterior mediastinum, arching over the right main bronchus to enter the superior vena cava.

Origin
Ascending lumbar and right subcostal veins
Course
Ascends on the right of the vertebral bodies in the posterior mediastinum
Termination
Arches forward over the right main bronchus into the superior vena cava
Tributaries
Posterior intercostal, hemiazygos, accessory hemiazygos, bronchial and oesophageal veins
Clinical relevance. It is the crucial collateral pathway between the superior and inferior vena cava — in IVC or SVC obstruction it enlarges dramatically and keeps venous return going. It lies directly in the operative field of anterior thoracic spine approaches.

Nerves

Phrenic Nerve (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Nervus phrenicus · Nerve · Peripheral Nervous System

Arises from C3, C4 and C5, descends vertically on the anterior surface of scalenus anterior, enters the thorax and runs over the pericardium to the diaphragm — the sole motor supply to each hemidiaphragm.

Roots
C3, C4, C5 — "C3, 4, 5 keeps the diaphragm alive"
Course
Down the anterior surface of scalenus anterior, between subclavian artery and vein, over the pericardium
Motor
The whole of its own hemidiaphragm
Sensory
Central diaphragm, pericardium, mediastinal pleura
Clinical relevance. Because it carries sensation from the central diaphragm, irritation there refers pain to the shoulder tip (C4 dermatome) — the Kehr sign of splenic rupture or diaphragmatic blood. It is also the reason a high cervical cord injury above C3 abolishes spontaneous breathing, whereas a C5–C6 injury spares it. Interscalene block reliably causes temporary hemidiaphragm palsy.

Lymphatics

Thoracic Duct & Cisterna Chyli

Ductus thoracicus · Lymphatics · Immune System

The principal lymphatic channel of the body, draining lymph from everywhere except the right upper quadrant. It begins as the cisterna chyli at L1–L2, ascends through the thorax, and empties into the junction of the left internal jugular and left subclavian veins.

Origin
Cisterna chyli at L1–L2, anterior to the vertebral bodies
Course
Through the aortic hiatus, ascends in the posterior mediastinum, crosses to the left at T5
Termination
Left venous angle (junction of left internal jugular and left subclavian veins)
Drainage
The whole body except the right head/neck, right upper limb and right thorax
Clinical relevance. Injury during thoracic or cervical spine surgery causes a chylous leak or chylothorax — a milky drain output that requires dietary modification (medium-chain triglycerides) or ligation. The left supraclavicular (Virchow) node draining here can be the first sign of abdominal malignancy.

Cisterna Chyli

Cisterna chyli · Lymphatics · Immune System

A dilated lymphatic sac in front of the L1–L2 vertebral bodies, to the right of the aorta, where the intestinal and lumbar lymph trunks converge before the thoracic duct begins.

Location
Anterior to L1–L2, right of the abdominal aorta, behind the right crus of the diaphragm
Receives
Right and left lumbar trunks and the intestinal trunk
Drains into
Thoracic duct, which ascends through the aortic hiatus
Contents
Chyle — lymph rich in absorbed dietary fat, milky after a meal
Clinical relevance. It sits directly in the operative field of anterior lumbar interbody fusion and of aortic surgery. Injury produces chylous ascites or a chylous fistula — a milky drain output requiring a medium-chain triglyceride diet or ligation.

Lymphatic Duct

Ductus lymphaticus dexter · Lymphatics · Immune System

A short vessel, roughly 1 cm long, formed by the right jugular, subclavian and bronchomediastinal trunks. It drains the right upper quadrant of the body into the right venous angle.

Formation
Right jugular, subclavian and bronchomediastinal lymph trunks
Termination
Right venous angle — junction of the right internal jugular and subclavian veins
Drainage
Right side of the head and neck, right upper limb, right thorax and the right side of the heart and lungs
Length
About 1 cm
Clinical relevance. It is the mirror of the thoracic duct, which drains the entire rest of the body. This asymmetry is why a left supraclavicular node (Virchow) signals abdominal malignancy, while right supraclavicular nodes point to thoracic or right upper limb disease.

Mediastinal & Hilar Nodes (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Nodi lymphoidei mediastinales · Lymphatics · Immune System

Node groups around the trachea, bronchi and great vessels — paratracheal, tracheobronchial and hilar — draining the lungs, oesophagus, heart and mediastinal structures.

Groups
Paratracheal, tracheobronchial (carinal), bronchopulmonary (hilar), anterior and posterior mediastinal
Afferents
Lungs, pleura, heart, oesophagus, thoracic wall
Efferents
Bronchomediastinal trunks → thoracic duct (left) or right lymphatic duct
Access
Sampled by mediastinoscopy or endobronchial ultrasound
Clinical relevance. Mediastinal nodal status determines staging and operability in lung cancer. In the orthopaedic context, hilar and mediastinal lymphadenopathy on a chest film in a patient with bone lesions raises sarcoidosis, tuberculosis or metastatic disease.

Parasternal (Internal Mammary) Nodes (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Nodi lymphoidei parasternales · Lymphatics · Immune System

A chain running alongside the internal thoracic vessels just lateral to the sternum, draining the medial breast, the anterior chest wall and the upper anterior abdominal wall.

Location
Along the internal thoracic artery, in the intercostal spaces beside the sternum
Afferents
Medial quadrants of the breast, anterior chest and upper abdominal wall, diaphragm
Efferents
Bronchomediastinal trunk
Imaging
Assessed by ultrasound, MRI or sentinel node scintigraphy
Clinical relevance. Roughly a quarter of breast lymph drains medially to these nodes rather than to the axilla, so a medial-quadrant tumour can metastasise with a clear axilla. Involvement upstages the disease and alters radiotherapy planning.

Intercostal & Diaphragmatic Nodes (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Nodi lymphoidei intercostales · Lymphatics · Immune System

Small nodes lying in the posterior intercostal spaces near the rib heads, together with the nodes on the superior surface of the diaphragm, draining the posterolateral chest wall and pleura.

Location
Posterior ends of the intercostal spaces, beside the vertebral column; and on the diaphragm
Afferents
Posterolateral chest wall, parietal pleura, posterior diaphragm, vertebrae
Efferents
Thoracic duct directly, or via the para-aortic chain
Note
Among the few node groups draining straight into the thoracic duct
Clinical relevance. They provide a direct route by which posterior chest wall and vertebral disease can spread without passing through the mediastinal nodes — worth remembering when staging a spinal or chest wall tumour that looks localised on imaging.
