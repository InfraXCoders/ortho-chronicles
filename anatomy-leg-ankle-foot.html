Leg, Ankle & Foot Anatomy

Bones, muscles, ligaments, vessels and nerves — with origins, insertions, nerve supply, actions and the clinical points that matter, linked to a 3D model you can rotate.

Dr. Maninder SinghMS Orthopedics | Associate Professor, GMCH Amritsar
November 2026 14 min read

The leg is divided into four compartments — anterior, lateral, superficial posterior and deep posterior — each with its own muscles, artery and nerve. That compartmental anatomy explains compartment syndrome after tibial fractures and nerve palsies such as foot drop from common peroneal injury. At the ankle, the talus transmits body weight, the mortise is stabilised by the syndesmosis and collateral ligaments, and the foot’s arches are supported by the plantar fascia and spring ligament.

Bones

Tibia (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Tibia · Bone · Lower Limb

The shin bone and the weight-bearing bone of the leg — the second longest bone in the body. It carries essentially all the load from knee to ankle, with the fibula serving mainly as a muscle attachment and lateral ankle strut. Its anteromedial surface lies directly under the skin, which explains both its accessibility and its notoriety: open fractures and slow healing are common.

Articulations

  • Knee joint (femoral condyles, menisci interposed)
  • Superior and inferior tibiofibular joints
  • Ankle (talocrural) joint

Muscle & ligament attachments

  • Patellar tendon (tibial tuberosity)
  • Pes anserinus: sartorius, gracilis, semitendinosus (anteromedial)
  • Iliotibial band (Gerdy tubercle)
  • Tibialis anterior and posterior; soleus

Key landmarks

  • Medial tibial plateau — Concave and larger, matching the medial femoral condyle — the compartment most often destroyed in varus knee arthritis.
  • Lateral tibial plateau — Convex and higher; depressed fractures here result from valgus impaction ("bumper" injuries).
  • Tibial tuberosity — Insertion of the patellar tendon. Traction apophysitis here in adolescents is Osgood-Schlatter disease.
  • Tibial spines — Intercondylar eminence giving attachment to the cruciate ligaments; avulsion here is the paediatric equivalent of an ACL tear.
  • Gerdy's tubercle — Insertion of the iliotibial band on the anterolateral tibia — a standard landmark for lateral knee approaches.
  • Subcutaneous border — The palpable shin. Minimal soft-tissue cover here means fractures are frequently open and skin necrosis is a real risk.
  • Medial malleolus — The medial buttress of the ankle mortise and anchor of the deltoid ligament.
  • Plafond — The distal articular surface (tibial ceiling) of the ankle joint — high-energy axial injuries here are pilon fractures.
Tibial shaft fracture. The slowest-healing common long bone (12–24 weeks) because of poor blood supply and thin soft-tissue cover. Smoking cessation is genuinely part of the treatment plan — nicotine can double healing time or cause non-union.
Compartment syndrome. Most commonly follows tibial fracture. Pain out of proportion to the injury and severe pain on passive toe extension are the earliest signs — pulses are present until very late. Diagnosis is clinical; treatment is immediate fasciotomy.
Tibial plateau fracture. Restoring articular congruity and mechanical axis prevents early post-traumatic arthritis. CT is essential for planning; the soft-tissue envelope often dictates the timing of surgery.

Fibula (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Fibula · Bone · Lower Limb

A slender lateral strut carrying only about 10–15% of the load through the leg. Its principal roles are muscle attachment and, distally, forming the lateral wall of the ankle mortise — a function so important that fibular length and rotation must be restored precisely in ankle fractures. Its expendable shaft makes it the standard donor for vascularised bone grafts.

Articulations

  • Superior and inferior tibiofibular joints
  • Ankle joint (lateral malleolus with talus)

Muscle & ligament attachments

  • Biceps femoris and LCL (head)
  • Peroneus longus and brevis
  • Extensor digitorum longus, extensor hallucis longus
  • Soleus and flexor hallucis longus (posterior)

Key landmarks

  • Fibular head — Attachment of the lateral collateral ligament and biceps femoris. The common peroneal nerve winds around the neck just below — the most frequently injured nerve in the lower limb.
  • Neck of fibula — The common peroneal nerve is subcutaneous here: tight plaster, prolonged leg crossing or a poorly padded table can cause foot drop.
  • Lateral malleolus — Extends about 1 cm more distal than the medial malleolus and forms the lateral wall of the mortise; the commonest ankle fracture site (Weber classification).
  • Interosseous border — Attachment of the interosseous membrane, which binds tibia and fibula and transfers load between them.
Weber classification. Fracture level relative to the syndesmosis predicts stability: Weber A (below) is usually stable; B (at) is variable; C (above) implies syndesmotic disruption and generally needs fixation.
Maisonneuve fracture. A proximal fibular fracture with a medial-sided ankle injury and syndesmotic rupture. Always palpate the whole fibula in an ankle injury — the proximal fracture is easily missed.
Free fibular graft. Up to ~25 cm of mid-shaft fibula can be harvested as a vascularised graft for reconstructing large bone defects, preserving 6 cm distally to protect ankle stability.

Talus (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Talus · Bone · Tarsus

The bone that transmits the entire body weight from leg to foot, gripped within the ankle mortise. Uniquely, no muscle attaches to it at all — roughly 60% of its surface is articular cartilage.

Articulations

  • Subtalar, midtarsal and tarsometatarsal joints
Clinical relevance. Its blood supply is largely retrograde, entering distally, so displaced talar neck fractures carry a high risk of avascular necrosis (Hawkins classification). Urgent anatomical reduction is essential.

Calcaneus (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Calcaneus · Bone · Tarsus

The largest tarsal bone, forming the heel and the posterior weight-bearing platform of the foot. It carries the Achilles insertion behind and supports the talus above through three facets, including the shelf-like sustentaculum tali.

Articulations

  • Subtalar, midtarsal and tarsometatarsal joints
Clinical relevance. Fractured by axial loading — a fall from height. Always examine the lumbar spine and the opposite heel, as associated vertebral fractures are common. Restoring Böhler angle and the subtalar joint surface determines long-term function.

Navicular (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Os naviculare · Bone · Tarsus

Boat-shaped bone of the medial column, lying between the talar head and the cuneiforms. Its tuberosity is the main insertion of tibialis posterior, the chief dynamic support of the medial longitudinal arch.

Articulations

  • Subtalar, midtarsal and tarsometatarsal joints
Clinical relevance. Navicular stress fracture is a classic overuse injury in running and jumping athletes — slow to heal and often invisible on plain films, so CT or MRI is needed. An accessory navicular can cause a painful medial prominence.

Cuboid (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Os cuboideum · Bone · Tarsus

The lateral column bone, articulating with the calcaneus behind and the fourth and fifth metatarsals in front. Its plantar groove carries the peroneus longus tendon into the sole.

Articulations

  • Subtalar, midtarsal and tarsometatarsal joints
Clinical relevance. Isolated fracture is rare; a crushed cuboid ("nutcracker" fracture) shortens the lateral column and must be restored to length to avoid a painful, deformed midfoot.

Cuneiform Bones (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Ossa cuneiformia · Bone · Tarsus

Three wedge-shaped bones — medial, intermediate and lateral — whose shape locks the transverse arch of the midfoot. The recessed second metatarsal base between them forms the keystone of the Lisfranc joint.

Articulations

  • Subtalar, midtarsal and tarsometatarsal joints
Clinical relevance. Lisfranc injury — disruption between the medial cuneiform and the second metatarsal base — is missed in up to 20% of cases and is a leading cause of long-term midfoot disability. Weight-bearing X-rays or CT are required if suspected.

Foot & Ankle (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Ossa pedis · Bone · Lower Limb

Twenty-six bones arranged into three functional arches — two longitudinal and one transverse — supported by the plantar fascia, the spring ligament and the intrinsic muscles. This architecture makes the foot a shock absorber at heel strike and a rigid lever at toe-off, absorbing several times body weight with every stride.

Articulations

  • Ankle (talocrural) joint
  • Subtalar joint (inversion/eversion)
  • Midtarsal, tarsometatarsal, MTP and IP joints

Muscle & ligament attachments

  • Achilles tendon (calcaneal tuberosity)
  • Tibialis posterior and anterior
  • Peroneal tendons
  • Plantar fascia and intrinsic foot muscles

Key landmarks

  • Calcaneus — The largest tarsal bone and the heel's weight-bearing platform. Fractures follow axial loading (a fall from height) — always examine the spine too, as associated lumbar fractures are common.
  • Talus — Transmits the entire body weight to the foot yet has no muscular attachments at all. Its retrograde blood supply makes avascular necrosis after talar neck fracture a genuine risk.
  • Navicular tuberosity — Palpable medial prominence and insertion of tibialis posterior, the chief dynamic supporter of the medial arch.
  • Base of 5th metatarsal — Peroneus brevis inserts here. Distinguish an avulsion fracture (heals well) from a Jones fracture at the metaphyseal-diaphyseal junction, which is prone to non-union.
  • Sustentaculum tali — A medial calcaneal shelf supporting the talus, traversed by the flexor hallucis longus tendon; a constant fragment in calcaneal fracture fixation.
  • Plantar fascia origin — The medial calcaneal tuberosity — the site of pain in plantar fasciitis, classically worst with the first steps in the morning.
  • Ankle mortise — The talus gripped between medial and lateral malleoli. Even 1 mm of talar shift reduces contact area by around 40% and leads to arthritis — anatomical reduction is non-negotiable.
Plantar fasciitis. Load-related degeneration of the fascia, not inflammation. Around 90% settle within a year with calf and fascia stretching, heel cups and load management. The heel spur seen on X-ray is a bystander, not the cause.
Achilles tendon rupture. A sudden "kick to the back of the leg" with a palpable gap and a positive Thompson test. Both operative and non-operative functional rehabilitation give good results; early functional bracing matters more than the choice itself.
Diabetic foot. Neuropathy plus vascular disease means ulcers form painlessly and infection spreads silently. Every diabetic foot needs regular inspection, protective footwear and prompt treatment of any break in the skin.

Muscles

Gastrocnemius & Soleus (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Triceps surae · Muscle · Musculoskeletal

The calf complex: the two-headed gastrocnemius crossing both knee and ankle, and the deeper, broader soleus crossing only the ankle. Together they form the Achilles tendon — the strongest tendon in the body, transmitting forces up to ten times body weight.

Origin
Gastrocnemius — femoral condyles; soleus — posterior tibia and fibula
Insertion
Calcaneal tuberosity via the Achilles tendon
Nerve
Tibial nerve (S1, S2)
Action
Ankle plantarflexion; gastrocnemius also assists knee flexion
Blood supply
Sural arteries from the popliteal; soleus also from the posterior tibial and peroneal.
Venous drainage
Soleal venous sinuses and venae comitantes to the popliteal vein.
Lymphatic drainage
Popliteal nodes to the deep inguinal group.
Clinical relevance. The soleus is the "peripheral heart", pumping venous blood back from the leg — immobility here is a key factor in deep vein thrombosis. The Achilles has a relatively avascular zone 2–6 cm above its insertion, which is where degeneration and rupture typically occur.

Tibialis Anterior (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Musculus tibialis anterior · Muscle · Musculoskeletal

The strongest dorsiflexor of the ankle, running down the front of the shin and crossing to the medial side of the foot. It lifts the foot in swing phase and controls its lowering after heel strike.

Origin
Lateral condyle and upper lateral surface of the tibia, interosseous membrane
Insertion
Medial cuneiform and base of the first metatarsal
Nerve
Deep peroneal nerve (L4, L5)
Action
Dorsiflexion and inversion of the foot
Blood supply
Anterior tibial artery.
Venous drainage
Anterior tibial venae comitantes to the popliteal vein.
Lymphatic drainage
Popliteal to deep inguinal nodes.
Clinical relevance. Loss of this muscle — usually through common peroneal nerve palsy at the fibular neck — causes foot drop and a high-stepping gait. It is also the key muscle of the anterior compartment, the compartment most often affected by compartment syndrome after tibial fracture.

Tibialis Posterior (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Musculus tibialis posterior · Muscle · Musculoskeletal

The deepest muscle of the posterior compartment, its tendon passing behind the medial malleolus to fan out onto the navicular and the plantar tarsus — the chief dynamic support of the medial longitudinal arch.

Origin
Posterior tibia, fibula and interosseous membrane
Insertion
Navicular tuberosity, cuneiforms and bases of metatarsals 2–4
Nerve
Tibial nerve (L4, L5)
Action
Plantarflexion and inversion of the foot; supports the medial arch
Blood supply
Posterior tibial and peroneal arteries.
Venous drainage
Posterior tibial venae comitantes to the popliteal vein.
Lymphatic drainage
Popliteal to deep inguinal nodes.
Clinical relevance. Tibialis posterior tendon dysfunction is the commonest cause of acquired adult flatfoot: the arch collapses, the hindfoot goes into valgus and the patient cannot perform a single-leg heel raise. Early bracing and strengthening can prevent a rigid deformity requiring reconstruction.

Peroneus Longus & Brevis (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Musculi fibulares · Muscle · Musculoskeletal

The lateral compartment muscles, their tendons hooking behind the lateral malleolus. Brevis inserts on the fifth metatarsal base; longus crosses the sole to the medial cuneiform and first metatarsal.

Origin
Lateral surface of the fibula
Insertion
Brevis — base of the 5th metatarsal; Longus — medial cuneiform and 1st metatarsal base
Nerve
Superficial peroneal nerve (L5, S1)
Action
Eversion of the foot; assist plantarflexion; longus supports the transverse arch
Blood supply
Peroneal (fibular) artery with anterior tibial branches.
Venous drainage
Corresponding venae comitantes.
Lymphatic drainage
Popliteal to deep inguinal nodes.
Clinical relevance. Peroneus brevis avulses the fifth metatarsal base in inversion injuries — distinguish this from a Jones fracture, which lies more distally and is prone to non-union. Peroneal tendon subluxation behind the lateral malleolus causes persistent lateral ankle pain after a "sprain".

Extensor Digitorum & Hallucis Longus (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

M. extensor digitorum longus et hallucis longus · Muscle · Musculoskeletal

The long toe extensors of the anterior compartment, running with tibialis anterior beneath the extensor retinaculum onto the toes.

Origin
Anterior fibula, interosseous membrane and lateral tibial condyle
Insertion
Extensor expansions of the toes; EHL onto the distal phalanx of the great toe
Nerve
Deep peroneal nerve (L5, S1)
Action
Toe extension and ankle dorsiflexion; EDL assists eversion
Blood supply
Anterior tibial artery.
Venous drainage
Anterior tibial veins.
Lymphatic drainage
Popliteal to deep inguinal nodes.
Clinical relevance. These share the anterior compartment with tibialis anterior and the deep peroneal nerve, so pain on *passive toe flexion* is an early and sensitive sign of anterior compartment syndrome after tibial fracture. Weak great-toe extension is the classic motor sign of an L5 radiculopathy.

Flexor Digitorum & Hallucis Longus (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

M. flexor digitorum longus et hallucis longus · Muscle · Musculoskeletal

The deep posterior compartment toe flexors, passing with tibialis posterior behind the medial malleolus into the sole of the foot.

Origin
Posterior tibia (FDL) and posterior fibula (FHL)
Insertion
Distal phalanges — FDL to toes 2–5, FHL to the great toe
Nerve
Tibial nerve (S1–S3)
Action
Toe flexion; assist plantarflexion and inversion; stabilise the arch at toe-off
Blood supply
Posterior tibial artery (flexor digitorum longus) and peroneal artery (flexor hallucis longus).
Venous drainage
Posterior tibial and peroneal veins.
Lymphatic drainage
Popliteal to deep inguinal nodes.
Clinical relevance. Their tendons run behind the medial malleolus in the tarsal tunnel with the tibial nerve — the mnemonic "Tom, Dick and a Very Nervous Harry" gives the order. FDL is the standard tendon transfer used to reconstruct a failed tibialis posterior in adult flatfoot.

Tendons & ligaments

Achilles (Calcaneal) Tendon (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Tendo calcaneus · Tendon · Connective Tissue

The thickest and strongest tendon in the body, formed by the union of the gastrocnemius and soleus aponeuroses and inserting into the posterior calcaneus. It transmits forces of up to ten times body weight in running and jumping, storing and returning elastic energy with every stride.

Origin
Confluence of the gastrocnemius and soleus aponeuroses
Insertion
Middle third of the posterior calcaneal tuberosity
Blood supply
Relatively avascular watershed zone 2–6 cm above the insertion
Action
Ankle plantarflexion; elastic energy return in gait
Clinical relevance. Rupture occurs typically in that hypovascular zone 2–6 cm above the insertion, in a middle-aged "weekend athlete", with a palpable gap and a positive Thompson test. Fluoroquinolone antibiotics and local steroid injection both raise the risk. Functional rehabilitation matters more than the choice between operative and non-operative treatment.

Interosseous Membrane of Leg (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Membrana interossea cruris · Tendon · Connective Tissue

The fibrous sheet uniting tibia and fibula, continuous below with the interosseous ligament of the syndesmosis. It divides the leg into anterior and posterior compartments and is pierced proximally by the anterior tibial vessels.

Attachments
Interosseous borders of the tibia and fibula
Function
Binds the tibia and fibula; separates the leg compartments; transfers load to the fibula
Distal continuation
Interosseous ligament of the inferior tibiofibular syndesmosis
Perforated by
Anterior tibial artery proximally; perforating peroneal artery distally
Clinical relevance. Its distal continuation is the syndesmosis, disruption of which defines the unstable ankle fracture and mandates fixation. A Maisonneuve injury tears this membrane along its length, which is why a proximal fibular fracture must be sought in any medial-sided ankle injury.

Long Plantar Ligament (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Ligamentum plantare longum · Tendon · Connective Tissue

The longest ligament of the foot, running from the plantar calcaneus forward to the cuboid and the bases of the middle metatarsals, forming a tunnel for the peroneus longus tendon.

Attachments
Plantar surface of the calcaneus to the cuboid and metatarsal bases 2–4
Function
Principal passive support of the lateral longitudinal arch
Forms
A tunnel converting the cuboid groove into a canal for peroneus longus
Deep to it
Short plantar (calcaneocuboid) ligament
Clinical relevance. With the plantar fascia and spring ligament it holds up the longitudinal arch — failure of this passive support, alongside tibialis posterior dysfunction, produces acquired adult flatfoot.

Arteries

Anterior & Posterior Tibial Arteries (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

A. tibialis anterior et posterior · Artery · Cardiovascular

The terminal branches of the popliteal artery supplying the leg and foot. The anterior tibial artery pierces the interosseous membrane into the anterior compartment and becomes dorsalis pedis; the posterior tibial artery runs behind the medial malleolus into the sole.

Origin
Popliteal artery bifurcation at the lower border of popliteus
Course
Anterior tibial — anterior compartment with the deep peroneal nerve; Posterior tibial — deep posterior compartment with the tibial nerve
Termination
Dorsalis pedis (dorsum of the foot); medial and lateral plantar arteries (sole)
Surface
Dorsalis pedis — lateral to the extensor hallucis longus tendon; Posterior tibial — behind the medial malleolus
Clinical relevance. These two pulses are the routine assessment of distal perfusion in leg trauma, in the diabetic foot and in peripheral vascular disease. Absent pulses after a tibial fracture demand urgent vascular assessment — but remember that compartment syndrome can be present with entirely normal pulses.

Dorsalis Pedis Artery (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Arteria dorsalis pedis · Artery · Cardiovascular

The continuation of the anterior tibial artery onto the dorsum of the foot, running lateral to the extensor hallucis longus tendon before diving into the sole to complete the plantar arch.

Origin
Continuation of the anterior tibial artery at the ankle
Course
Dorsum of the foot, lateral to the EHL tendon, medial to the deep peroneal nerve
Termination
Deep plantar artery completing the plantar arch
Surface
Palpable between the first and second metatarsal bases
Clinical relevance. A routinely checked pulse in the diabetic foot, in peripheral arterial disease and after tibial or ankle trauma. Note it is congenitally absent in up to 10% of normal people, so an absent pulse must be interpreted alongside the posterior tibial pulse and the overall perfusion.

Peroneal (Fibular) Artery (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Arteria fibularis · Artery · Cardiovascular

A branch of the posterior tibial artery running down the deep posterior compartment close to the fibula, supplying the lateral leg and giving the nutrient artery to the fibula.

Origin
Posterior tibial artery, just below the popliteus
Course
Deep posterior compartment, adjacent to the medial fibular crest
Supplies
Fibula, lateral compartment muscles, ankle anastomosis
Termination
Perforating and communicating branches around the ankle
Clinical relevance. It is the pedicle of the vascularised free fibular graft used to reconstruct large bone defects — the reason fibula can be harvested at all. It can also be the dominant supply to the foot in peripheral vascular disease, so it must be assessed before graft harvest.

Veins

Small Saphenous Vein (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Vena saphena parva · Vein · Cardiovascular

A superficial vein ascending from behind the lateral malleolus along the midline of the calf, piercing the deep fascia to drain into the popliteal vein in the popliteal fossa.

Origin
Lateral end of the dorsal venous arch of the foot
Course
Behind the lateral malleolus, then up the midline of the posterior calf
Termination
Popliteal vein at the saphenopopliteal junction
Relations
Accompanied by the sural nerve
Clinical relevance. The sural nerve runs with it and is at risk during vein stripping or short saphenous surgery, and during posterolateral approaches to the Achilles tendon. Thrombosis here can propagate into the popliteal vein and become a genuine embolic risk.

Deep Calf Veins & Perforators (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Venae perforantes · Vein · Cardiovascular

The venous sinusoids within soleus and gastrocnemius, linked to the superficial system by valved perforating veins. Muscle contraction squeezes them, driving blood proximally — the calf muscle pump.

Components
Soleal and gastrocnemius venous sinuses; posterior tibial and peroneal veins
Perforators
Valved channels directing flow from superficial to deep
Mechanism
Muscle contraction plus one-way valves propel blood toward the heart
Function
Chief mechanism of venous return from the lower limb against gravity
Clinical relevance. This is the "peripheral heart", and immobility switches it off — which is precisely why deep vein thrombosis is such a risk after lower limb surgery, casting and long-haul travel. Early mobilisation, calf exercises and intermittent compression devices work by restoring this pump. Valve failure in the perforators causes chronic venous insufficiency and ulceration.

Nerves

Common Peroneal & Tibial Nerves (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

N. fibularis communis et N. tibialis · Nerve · Peripheral Nervous System

The two terminal divisions of the sciatic nerve. The common peroneal winds around the neck of the fibula to supply the anterior and lateral compartments; the tibial nerve continues down the back of the leg into the sole of the foot.

Common peroneal
Winds around the fibular neck; divides into superficial (evertors, lateral leg sensation) and deep (dorsiflexors, first web space) branches
Tibial
Deep posterior compartment; passes behind the medial malleolus in the tarsal tunnel to become the plantar nerves
Motor
Peroneal — dorsiflexion and eversion; Tibial — plantarflexion, inversion and the foot intrinsics
Sensory
Peroneal — dorsum of the foot; Tibial — sole of the foot
Clinical relevance. The common peroneal nerve is the most frequently injured nerve in the lower limb because it lies subcutaneously against the fibular neck. Tight plaster, prolonged leg crossing, a poorly padded operating table or fibular neck fracture all cause foot drop — pad the fibular neck in every leg cast and on every table.

Deep & Superficial Peroneal Nerves (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

N. fibularis profundus et superficialis · Nerve · Peripheral Nervous System

The two divisions of the common peroneal nerve. The deep branch runs with the anterior tibial vessels supplying the dorsiflexors and the first web space; the superficial branch supplies the evertors and most of the dorsum of the foot.

Deep
Anterior compartment — tibialis anterior, EHL, EDL; sensation to the first web space
Superficial
Lateral compartment — peroneus longus and brevis; sensation to most of the dorsum of the foot
Origin
Bifurcation of the common peroneal nerve at the fibular neck
Course
Deep branch pierces the interosseous membrane into the anterior compartment
Clinical relevance. First web space sensation is the specific test of the deep peroneal nerve and is the earliest sensory sign in anterior compartment syndrome. The superficial nerve pierces the deep fascia about 10 cm above the lateral malleolus and is at risk in lateral approaches to the fibula and in ankle arthroscopy portals.

Sural Nerve (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Nervus suralis · Nerve · Peripheral Nervous System

A purely sensory nerve formed by branches of the tibial and common peroneal nerves, descending with the small saphenous vein behind the lateral malleolus to supply the lateral foot.

Formation
Medial sural cutaneous (tibial) + peroneal communicating branch
Course
Down the midline of the calf, then behind the lateral malleolus with the small saphenous vein
Sensory
Posterolateral leg and lateral border of the foot to the little toe
Motor
None
Clinical relevance. It is the standard donor for nerve biopsy and for nerve grafting, precisely because losing it costs only a small patch of sensation. It is at risk in the posterolateral approach to the Achilles tendon, percutaneous Achilles repair and short saphenous vein surgery.

Medial & Lateral Plantar Nerves (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Nn. plantares medialis et lateralis · Nerve · Peripheral Nervous System

The terminal branches of the tibial nerve, dividing beneath the flexor retinaculum and running forward in the sole — the plantar equivalents of the median and ulnar nerves in the hand.

Origin
Tibial nerve, within the tarsal tunnel
Motor
Medial — abductor hallucis, FDB, FHB; Lateral — the remaining intrinsic foot muscles
Sensory
Medial — medial sole and medial 3½ toes; Lateral — lateral sole and lateral 1½ toes
Course
Deep to the flexor retinaculum, then between the intrinsic muscle layers
Clinical relevance. Compression in the tarsal tunnel causes burning sole pain and numbness, worse on standing, with a positive Tinel sign behind the medial malleolus. Entrapment of the first branch of the lateral plantar nerve (Baxter nerve) is an under-recognised cause of chronic heel pain that mimics plantar fasciitis but does not settle with fascia treatment.
