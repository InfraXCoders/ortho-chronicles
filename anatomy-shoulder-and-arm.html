Shoulder & Upper Arm Anatomy

Bones, muscles, ligaments, vessels and nerves — with origins, insertions, nerve supply, actions and the clinical points that matter, linked to a 3D model you can rotate.

Dr. Maninder SinghMS Orthopedics | Associate Professor, GMCH Amritsar
November 2026 19 min read

The shoulder girdle is built for range, not stability: a shallow glenoid socket receives the humeral head, held in place mainly by muscles rather than bone. The scapula glides over the chest wall, the clavicle acts as its strut, and the rotator cuff tendons steer the humeral head while the deltoid lifts the arm. Understanding which muscle is supplied by which nerve, and where the axillary artery and brachial plexus run, explains most shoulder injuries — rotator cuff tears, dislocations that injure the axillary nerve, clavicle fractures and frozen shoulder.

Bones

Clavicle (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Clavicula · Bone · Shoulder Girdle

The clavicle is the only bony strut connecting the upper limb to the axial skeleton, holding the shoulder laterally so the arm can swing free of the trunk. Its S-shape and subcutaneous position make it the most commonly fractured bone in the body, and it is the first bone to ossify and the last to fuse (around age 25).

Articulations

  • Sternoclavicular joint (medially)
  • Acromioclavicular joint (laterally)

Muscle & ligament attachments

  • Pectoralis major, deltoid (anterior)
  • Trapezius (posterior)
  • Sternocleidomastoid (superomedial)
  • Subclavius (inferior groove)

Key landmarks

  • Sternoclavicular joint — The only true joint between upper limb and axial skeleton — a saddle joint with an intra-articular disc; posterior dislocation is rare but can compress the great vessels and trachea.
  • Mid-shaft — Junction of the medial and lateral curves — the weakest point and the site of ~80% of clavicle fractures.
  • Conoid tubercle — Attachment of the conoid part of the coracoclavicular ligament, the key vertical stabiliser of the AC joint.
  • Acromioclavicular joint — Plane synovial joint at the shoulder tip; injured in "shoulder separation" from a fall onto the point of the shoulder.
When to fix a clavicle. Most mid-shaft fractures heal in a sling. Plate fixation is considered for shortening over 2 cm, marked comminution, skin tenting, open fracture or in throwing athletes — it roughly halves non-union risk in displaced fractures.
What lies beneath. The subclavian vessels and brachial plexus run directly under the middle third; a displaced fragment mandates careful distal neurovascular examination.

Scapula (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Scapula · Bone · Shoulder Girdle

A flat triangular bone suspended on the posterior chest wall entirely by muscle — it has no direct joint with the ribs. This muscular suspension gives it huge mobility: scapulothoracic motion supplies roughly one-third of total shoulder elevation (the 2:1 scapulohumeral rhythm). It provides the glenoid socket and the origin of the rotator cuff.

Articulations

  • Glenohumeral joint (with humerus)
  • Acromioclavicular joint
  • Scapulothoracic articulation (functional, not a true joint)

Muscle & ligament attachments

  • Rotator cuff: supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor, subscapularis
  • Deltoid (spine and acromion)
  • Trapezius, rhomboids, levator scapulae, serratus anterior
  • Long head of biceps (supraglenoid tubercle); long head of triceps (infraglenoid)

Key landmarks

  • Acromion — The bony point of the shoulder; articulates with the clavicle and forms the roof of the subacromial space. Its shape (Bigliani type III hooked) is associated with cuff impingement.
  • Coracoid process — The "lighthouse of the shoulder" — anterior guide to the brachial plexus and axillary vessels. Origin of short head of biceps and coracobrachialis; insertion of pectoralis minor.
  • Glenoid fossa — Shallow socket covering only about a third of the humeral head — great mobility at the cost of stability, deepened by the fibrocartilaginous labrum.
  • Spine of scapula — Palpable ridge dividing supraspinatus above from infraspinatus below; lies at the T3 level.
  • Inferior angle — The lowest point of the scapula, at the T7 level — used clinically to count ribs.
  • Suprascapular notch — Transmits the suprascapular nerve; entrapment here causes supraspinatus and infraspinatus wasting with poorly localised shoulder pain.
Winging of the scapula. Serratus anterior palsy from long thoracic nerve injury causes medial winging — the scapula lifts off the chest wall when pushing against a wall. Trapezius palsy (spinal accessory nerve) gives lateral winging.
Scapular fracture. Rare and requiring high energy: look hard for associated rib fractures, pneumothorax and pulmonary contusion, which cause more morbidity than the scapular fracture itself.

Humerus (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Humerus · Bone · Upper Limb

The longest bone of the upper limb, spanning shoulder to elbow. Proximally its near-spherical head sits in the shallow glenoid, giving the shoulder its exceptional range at the price of stability. Distally the trochlea and capitellum form the elbow. The radial nerve spirals around the posterior shaft in the radial groove — an anatomical relationship that dominates the clinical management of humeral fractures.

Articulations

  • Glenohumeral joint (proximally)
  • Elbow: humero-ulnar and humero-radial joints (distally)

Muscle & ligament attachments

  • Rotator cuff onto the tubercles
  • Deltoid (tuberosity), pectoralis major, latissimus dorsi, teres major (bicipital groove region)
  • Common flexor origin (medial epicondyle) and common extensor origin (lateral epicondyle)

Key landmarks

  • Humeral head — Retroverted about 30°, covered in hyaline cartilage; its blood supply comes largely from the anterior circumflex humeral artery, at risk in fracture-dislocations.
  • Greater tubercle — Insertion of supraspinatus, infraspinatus and teres minor. Isolated fracture here effectively behaves like a rotator cuff tear.
  • Lesser tubercle — Insertion of subscapularis; the bicipital groove between the tubercles carries the long head of biceps tendon.
  • Surgical neck — The narrow region below the tubercles — a common fracture site in the elderly, endangering the axillary nerve and posterior circumflex humeral artery.
  • Deltoid tuberosity — Rough V-shaped area at mid-shaft where deltoid inserts, midway down the lateral shaft.
  • Radial (spiral) groove — The radial nerve runs here against bare bone — mid-shaft fractures cause wrist drop in roughly 10–15% of cases.
  • Medial epicondyle — The ulnar nerve passes behind it in the cubital tunnel — the "funny bone". Common flexor origin of the forearm.
  • Lateral epicondyle — Common extensor origin — the site of pain in lateral epicondylitis ("tennis elbow").
Radial nerve palsy. Wrist drop after a mid-shaft humeral fracture is usually a neurapraxia that recovers over 3–4 months. Most are observed with a cock-up splint; exploration is reserved for palsy appearing *after* manipulation or for open injuries.
Supracondylar fracture in children. A paediatric emergency. Assess the radial pulse and hand perfusion immediately — a pale, pulseless hand demands urgent reduction. Untreated ischaemia leads to Volkmann contracture.
Axillary nerve. After shoulder dislocation or surgical neck fracture, always test sensation over the "regimental badge" area of the lateral shoulder and deltoid contraction.

Muscles

Deltoid (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Musculus deltoideus · Muscle · Musculoskeletal

A thick triangular cap over the shoulder, in three functional parts: anterior (flexion), middle (abduction) and posterior (extension). The middle fibres are multipennate — built for power — and take over abduction from supraspinatus after the first 15°.

Origin
Lateral clavicle, acromion and spine of scapula
Insertion
Deltoid tuberosity of the humerus
Nerve
Axillary nerve (C5, C6)
Action
Abduction of the arm; anterior fibres flex and medially rotate, posterior fibres extend and laterally rotate
Blood supply
Posterior circumflex humeral artery (chief), with the deltoid branch of the thoraco-acromial and the anterior circumflex humeral.
Venous drainage
Venae comitantes to the axillary vein.
Lymphatic drainage
Lateral and apical axillary nodes.
Clinical relevance. The axillary nerve is at risk in shoulder dislocation and surgical neck fractures — always test sensation over the "regimental badge" area and deltoid power. In cuff-deficient shoulders, a reverse replacement lets deltoid alone elevate the arm.

Supraspinatus (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Musculus supraspinatus · Muscle · Musculoskeletal

The uppermost rotator cuff muscle, running from the supraspinous fossa beneath the acromion to the greater tubercle. It initiates the first 15° of abduction and, throughout elevation, compresses the humeral head into the glenoid so deltoid can work.

Origin
Supraspinous fossa of the scapula
Insertion
Superior facet of the greater tubercle
Nerve
Suprascapular nerve (C5, C6)
Action
Initiates abduction; a primary dynamic stabiliser of the glenohumeral joint
Blood supply
Suprascapular artery, with the dorsal scapular.
Venous drainage
Suprascapular vein.
Lymphatic drainage
Apical axillary and supraclavicular nodes.
Clinical relevance. By far the most commonly torn cuff tendon. It passes through the narrow subacromial space, so a hooked (Bigliani III) acromion causes impingement. A painful arc between 60° and 120°, a positive empty-can test and night pain are the classic findings; repaired tendon needs about three months before loaded work.

Infraspinatus (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Musculus infraspinatus · Muscle · Musculoskeletal

The main external rotator of the shoulder, filling the infraspinous fossa below the scapular spine and inserting on the middle facet of the greater tubercle.

Origin
Infraspinous fossa of the scapula
Insertion
Middle facet of the greater tubercle
Nerve
Suprascapular nerve (C5, C6)
Action
External rotation of the arm; posterior stabiliser of the humeral head
Blood supply
Suprascapular and circumflex scapular arteries.
Venous drainage
Corresponding venae comitantes.
Lymphatic drainage
Subscapular (posterior) axillary nodes.
Clinical relevance. Infraspinatus wasting is visible as a hollow below the scapular spine and is a sign of suprascapular nerve entrapment (at the spinoglenoid notch) or a massive cuff tear. Loss of external rotation strength predicts poor overhead function.

Subscapularis (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Musculus subscapularis · Muscle · Musculoskeletal

The only rotator cuff muscle on the anterior (costal) surface of the scapula, and the sole internal rotator of the cuff. It is the anterior restraint against dislocation.

Origin
Subscapular fossa (costal surface of the scapula)
Insertion
Lesser tubercle of the humerus
Nerve
Upper and lower subscapular nerves (C5–C6)
Action
Internal rotation; primary anterior stabiliser of the glenohumeral joint
Blood supply
Subscapular artery and branches of the axillary artery.
Venous drainage
To the axillary vein.
Lymphatic drainage
Subscapular and central axillary nodes.
Clinical relevance. Tears are under-diagnosed. Test with the lift-off and belly-press tests. Subscapularis integrity is essential for anterior stability and must be repaired or managed carefully during the deltopectoral approach and shoulder replacement.

Teres Minor (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Musculus teres minor · Muscle · Musculoskeletal

The smallest cuff muscle, running from the lateral scapular border to the inferior facet of the greater tubercle, assisting external rotation.

Origin
Upper lateral (axillary) border of the scapula
Insertion
Inferior facet of the greater tubercle
Nerve
Axillary nerve (C5, C6)
Action
External rotation and inferior stabilisation of the humeral head
Blood supply
Circumflex scapular and posterior circumflex humeral arteries.
Venous drainage
Corresponding venae comitantes.
Lymphatic drainage
Subscapular (posterior) axillary nodes.
Clinical relevance. Usually spared in cuff tears, which matters: intact teres minor preserves external rotation and is a favourable factor before reverse shoulder replacement, whereas fatty infiltration of it predicts a poorer result.

Teres Major (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Musculus teres major · Muscle · Musculoskeletal

A thick strap from the inferior angle of the scapula to the humerus, functioning with latissimus dorsi as "lat's little helper" and forming the lower border of the axilla.

Origin
Inferior angle and lower lateral border of the scapula
Insertion
Medial lip of the bicipital groove
Nerve
Lower subscapular nerve (C6, C7)
Action
Adduction, extension and internal rotation of the arm
Blood supply
Circumflex scapular (subscapular) and thoracodorsal arteries.
Venous drainage
To the axillary vein.
Lymphatic drainage
Subscapular (posterior) axillary nodes.
Clinical relevance. Its lower border marks where the axillary artery becomes the brachial artery — a key landmark in vascular assessment and in surgical exposure of the upper arm.

Pectoralis Major (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Musculus pectoralis major · Muscle · Musculoskeletal

The broad fan of the anterior chest wall, with clavicular, sternocostal and abdominal heads whose fibres twist 180° before inserting — which is why it can both flex and extend the shoulder depending on starting position.

Origin
Medial clavicle, sternum, upper six costal cartilages
Insertion
Lateral lip of the bicipital groove of the humerus
Nerve
Medial and lateral pectoral nerves (C5–T1)
Action
Adduction, medial rotation and flexion of the arm; accessory muscle of respiration
Blood supply
Pectoral branch of the thoraco-acromial artery, the lateral thoracic and perforating branches of the internal thoracic.
Venous drainage
To the axillary and subclavian veins.
Lymphatic drainage
Pectoral (anterior) axillary and parasternal nodes.
Clinical relevance. Rupture occurs typically in weight-trainers during bench press, producing a sudden tearing sensation, bruising and loss of the anterior axillary fold. Surgical repair is recommended in active individuals.

Pectoralis Minor (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Musculus pectoralis minor · Muscle · Musculoskeletal

A small triangular muscle deep to pectoralis major, running from the third to fifth ribs up to the coracoid process. It is the surgical key to the axilla, dividing the axillary artery and the axillary nodes into three levels.

Origin
Anterior surfaces of ribs 3–5
Insertion
Coracoid process of the scapula
Nerve
Medial pectoral nerve (C8, T1)
Action
Protracts and depresses the scapula; assists forced inspiration
Blood supply
Pectoral branch of the thoraco-acromial and the lateral thoracic arteries.
Venous drainage
To the axillary vein.
Lymphatic drainage
Pectoral (anterior) and apical axillary nodes.
Clinical relevance. It divides the axillary artery into its three parts and the axillary lymph nodes into levels I–III, the framework used in breast cancer surgery. A tight pectoralis minor tilts the scapula anteriorly, contributing to shoulder impingement, and can compress the neurovascular bundle in pectoralis minor (thoracic outlet) syndrome.

Latissimus Dorsi (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Musculus latissimus dorsi · Muscle · Musculoskeletal

The largest muscle of the back, sweeping from the lower spine and iliac crest up to the humerus — the "climbing muscle" that pulls the body up toward a fixed arm.

Origin
Spinous processes T7–L5, thoracolumbar fascia, iliac crest, lower ribs
Insertion
Floor of the bicipital groove of the humerus
Nerve
Thoracodorsal nerve (C6–C8)
Action
Extension, adduction and medial rotation of the arm
Blood supply
Thoracodorsal artery (from the subscapular).
Venous drainage
Thoracodorsal vein to the axillary vein.
Lymphatic drainage
Posterior (subscapular) axillary nodes.
Clinical relevance. Its reliable pedicle makes the latissimus dorsi flap a workhorse in reconstructive surgery — for breast reconstruction and for covering large soft-tissue defects, including around the shoulder and chest wall.

Serratus Anterior (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Musculus serratus anterior · Muscle · Musculoskeletal

A fan of digitations from the upper ribs to the medial border of the scapula, holding the scapula against the chest wall and rotating it upward — the "boxer's muscle" of protraction.

Origin
Outer surfaces of ribs 1–8
Insertion
Medial border of the scapula (costal surface)
Nerve
Long thoracic nerve (C5, C6, C7)
Action
Protraction and upward rotation of the scapula; holds it to the thorax
Blood supply
Lateral thoracic artery, with the superior thoracic and thoracodorsal.
Venous drainage
To the axillary vein.
Lymphatic drainage
Central and lateral axillary nodes.
Clinical relevance. Long thoracic nerve injury — from axillary surgery, trauma or viral neuritis — causes medial winging of the scapula, obvious when the patient pushes against a wall, with loss of overhead elevation. Recovery is often slow but frequently spontaneous.

Subclavius (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Musculus subclavius · Muscle · Musculoskeletal

A small muscle running beneath the clavicle from the first rib to the clavicular groove, acting as a cushion between the clavicle and the underlying neurovascular bundle.

Origin
First rib at the costochondral junction
Insertion
Subclavian groove on the inferior surface of the clavicle
Nerve
Nerve to subclavius (C5, C6)
Action
Depresses and stabilises the clavicle at the sternoclavicular joint
Blood supply
Clavicular branch of the thoraco-acromial artery, with the suprascapular.
Venous drainage
To the subclavian vein.
Lymphatic drainage
Apical (infraclavicular) axillary nodes.
Clinical relevance. It is the anatomical buffer protecting the subclavian vessels and brachial plexus from a fractured clavicle — but hypertrophy or a fibrous band here contributes to costoclavicular thoracic outlet compression, classically in swimmers and rowers.

Rhomboid Major & Minor (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Musculi rhomboidei · Muscle · Musculoskeletal

Two parallel straps running obliquely from the lower cervical and upper thoracic spinous processes down to the medial border of the scapula, retracting and downwardly rotating it.

Origin
Rhomboid minor — C7–T1 spinous processes; major — T2–T5 spinous processes
Insertion
Medial border of the scapula, from the spine to the inferior angle
Nerve
Dorsal scapular nerve (C5)
Action
Retraction, elevation and downward rotation of the scapula
Blood supply
Dorsal scapular artery (deep branch of the transverse cervical).
Venous drainage
To the external jugular and vertebral venous plexus.
Lymphatic drainage
Deep cervical and axillary nodes.
Clinical relevance. Dorsal scapular nerve injury causes a subtle lateral drift of the scapula, weaker than trapezius palsy and easily missed. Rhomboid weakness allows the scapula to wing medially during a slow, controlled push-up and is a common driver of scapular dyskinesis in overhead athletes.

Trapezius (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Musculus trapezius · Muscle · Musculoskeletal

A large diamond-shaped superficial back muscle in three functional parts: upper fibres elevate the scapula, middle fibres retract it, lower fibres depress it and rotate the glenoid upward during arm elevation.

Origin
External occipital protuberance, ligamentum nuchae, spinous processes C7–T12
Insertion
Lateral clavicle, acromion, spine of scapula
Nerve
Spinal accessory nerve (CN XI), with proprioception from C3–C4
Action
Elevation, retraction, depression and upward rotation of the scapula
Blood supply
Superficial branch of the transverse cervical artery, with dorsal scapular and posterior intercostal contributions.
Venous drainage
To the external and internal jugular veins.
Lymphatic drainage
Occipital and deep cervical nodes.
Clinical relevance. Injury to the spinal accessory nerve — classically during posterior triangle lymph node biopsy — causes trapezius palsy with shoulder droop, lateral scapular winging and difficulty elevating the arm above shoulder height.

Levator Scapulae (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Musculus levator scapulae · Muscle · Musculoskeletal

A strap running from the upper cervical transverse processes down to the superior angle of the scapula, elevating the scapula and, with the rhomboids, rotating the glenoid downward.

Origin
Transverse processes of C1–C4
Insertion
Superior angle and upper medial border of the scapula
Nerve
Dorsal scapular nerve (C5) and direct branches from C3–C4
Action
Elevates the scapula; downwardly rotates the glenoid; side-bends the neck
Blood supply
Dorsal scapular and transverse cervical arteries.
Venous drainage
Corresponding venae comitantes.
Lymphatic drainage
Deep cervical nodes.
Clinical relevance. One of the most common sites of myofascial pain in desk workers, felt as a sharp ache at the top inner corner of the shoulder blade with a stiff neck. Because it also downwardly rotates the glenoid, a tight levator scapulae works directly against the upward rotation needed to raise the arm overhead.

Biceps Brachii (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Musculus biceps brachii · Muscle · Musculoskeletal

Two-headed flexor of the front of the arm. The long head arises inside the shoulder joint from the supraglenoid tubercle and runs in the bicipital groove; the short head arises from the coracoid. It is the most powerful supinator of the forearm.

Origin
Long head — supraglenoid tubercle; short head — coracoid process
Insertion
Radial tuberosity and bicipital aponeurosis
Nerve
Musculocutaneous nerve (C5, C6)
Action
Elbow flexion and — most powerfully — forearm supination
Blood supply
Muscular branches of the brachial artery; the long head also from the anterior circumflex humeral.
Venous drainage
Venae comitantes to the brachial and axillary veins.
Lymphatic drainage
Deltopectoral (infraclavicular) and lateral axillary nodes.
Clinical relevance. Rupture of the long head produces the classic "Popeye" deformity; in older patients this is usually treated non-operatively with little functional loss. Distal biceps rupture, by contrast, causes major supination weakness and is usually repaired.

Triceps Brachii (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Musculus triceps brachii · Muscle · Musculoskeletal

The sole extensor of the elbow, with three heads. The long head crosses the shoulder from the infraglenoid tubercle; the medial and lateral heads arise from the humeral shaft either side of the radial groove.

Origin
Long head — infraglenoid tubercle; lateral and medial heads — posterior humerus
Insertion
Olecranon process of the ulna
Nerve
Radial nerve (C6–C8)
Action
Elbow extension; the long head also extends and adducts the shoulder
Blood supply
Profunda brachii (deep brachial) artery; long head also by the posterior circumflex humeral.
Venous drainage
Venae comitantes to the brachial veins.
Lymphatic drainage
Lateral axillary nodes.
Clinical relevance. Triceps function is essential for crutch walking and for pushing up from a chair — a key consideration in rehabilitation planning after upper limb injury or in tetraplegia.

Tendons & ligaments

Supraspinatus Tendon (Rotator Cuff) (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Tendo musculi supraspinati · Tendon · Connective Tissue

The tendon of supraspinatus, passing beneath the acromion and coracoacromial arch to insert into the superior facet of the greater tuberosity. It is the most frequently torn component of the rotator cuff.

Course
Beneath the acromion and coracoacromial arch, above the humeral head
Insertion
Superior facet of the greater tuberosity of the humerus
Function
Initiates abduction; compresses and centres the humeral head in the glenoid
Blood supply
Hypovascular “critical zone” about 1 cm from the insertion
Clinical relevance. Degenerate tears begin in the hypovascular critical zone and cause a painful arc between 60° and 120° of abduction. Subacromial impingement, a positive Jobe (empty-can) test and weakness of abduction are the clinical hallmarks.

Arteries

Subclavian, Axillary & Brachial Artery (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

A. subclavia / axillaris / brachialis · Artery · Cardiovascular

The continuous arterial highway of the upper limb, renamed as it passes each landmark: subclavian (to the first rib), axillary (to the lower border of teres major), then brachial down the arm to the cubital fossa.

Course
Subclavian → axillary (at the outer border of the first rib) → brachial (at teres major)
Relations
Travels with the cords of the brachial plexus in the axilla; lies medial to the biceps tendon in the cubital fossa
Termination
Divides into radial and ulnar arteries in the cubital fossa
Surface
Brachial pulse — medial to the biceps tendon; the standard site for blood pressure measurement
Clinical relevance. The brachial artery is at risk in supracondylar humeral fractures in children — a pale, pulseless hand demands immediate reduction. Compression of the subclavian vessels between the clavicle and first rib produces thoracic outlet syndrome.

Circumflex Humeral Arteries (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Aa. circumflexae humeri · Artery · Cardiovascular

Anterior and posterior circumflex humeral arteries encircle the surgical neck of the humerus. The posterior vessel passes through the quadrangular space with the axillary nerve; the anterior gives the ascending branch that supplies the humeral head.

Origin
Third part of the axillary artery
Course
Encircle the surgical neck of the humerus; posterior passes through the quadrangular space
Supplies
Humeral head (mainly via the arcuate artery), deltoid, shoulder joint
Relations
Posterior artery accompanies the axillary nerve
Clinical relevance. Disruption of these vessels in a displaced surgical neck or head-splitting fracture risks avascular necrosis of the humeral head — a key factor in deciding between fixation and replacement. The posterior vessel and axillary nerve are both at risk in shoulder dislocation.

Profunda Brachii Artery (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Arteria profunda brachii · Artery · Cardiovascular

The largest branch of the brachial artery, accompanying the radial nerve into the radial (spiral) groove on the back of the humerus and contributing to the anastomosis around the elbow.

Origin
Brachial artery, just below teres major
Course
Enters the radial groove with the radial nerve
Termination
Divides into radial and middle collateral arteries at the elbow
Relations
Travels with the radial nerve against bare humeral shaft
Clinical relevance. Its intimate relationship with the radial nerve means mid-shaft humeral fractures threaten both together. The rich collateral anastomosis around the elbow protects the forearm even if the brachial artery is injured distal to this branch.

Veins

Cephalic & Basilic Veins (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Vena cephalica et basilica · Vein · Cardiovascular

The main superficial veins of the upper limb, running in the subcutaneous tissue: the cephalic on the lateral (radial) side and the basilic medially. They are connected at the elbow by the median cubital vein.

Course
Cephalic — lateral arm, then through the deltopectoral groove; Basilic — medial arm, piercing the deep fascia to become the axillary vein
Connection
Median cubital vein crosses the cubital fossa
Termination
Cephalic → axillary vein; Basilic → axillary vein
Surface
Median cubital vein is the standard venepuncture site
Clinical relevance. The median cubital vein is the workhorse of blood sampling and IV access — but it lies directly over the brachial artery and median nerve, separated only by the bicipital aponeurosis ("the grace of God" fascia). The cephalic vein is used for arteriovenous fistula formation in dialysis.

Brachiocephalic & Subclavian Veins (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

V. brachiocephalica et subclavia · Vein · Cardiovascular

The subclavian vein continues from the axillary vein at the outer border of the first rib and joins the internal jugular vein to form the brachiocephalic vein, which in turn forms the superior vena cava.

Course
Subclavian vein passes over the first rib anterior to the scalenus anterior muscle
Junction
Unites with the internal jugular at the venous angle
Termination
Right and left brachiocephalic veins join to form the superior vena cava
Relations
Separated from the subclavian artery and brachial plexus by scalenus anterior
Clinical relevance. The subclavian vein is a standard route for central venous access and for pacemaker leads — but the pleural dome lies immediately beneath, so pneumothorax is the classic complication. Compression here produces venous thoracic outlet syndrome with arm swelling (Paget–Schroetter syndrome).

Nerves

Brachial Plexus (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Plexus brachialis · Nerve · Peripheral Nervous System

The nerve network supplying the entire upper limb, formed from the C5–T1 ventral rami. Its organisation — roots, trunks, divisions, cords, branches — passes between the scalene muscles, over the first rib and under the clavicle into the axilla.

Roots
Ventral rami of C5, C6, C7, C8 and T1
Organisation
Roots → trunks (upper, middle, lower) → divisions → cords (lateral, posterior, medial) → terminal branches
Terminal
Musculocutaneous, axillary, radial, median and ulnar nerves
Relations
Passes between anterior and middle scalene, over the first rib, posterior to the clavicle
Clinical relevance. Erb palsy (C5–C6, upper trunk) produces the "waiter's tip" posture and follows shoulder dystocia or a fall onto the shoulder. Klumpke palsy (C8–T1) affects the hand intrinsics and may include Horner syndrome. In trauma, the presence of Horner syndrome or winged scapula suggests a poor-prognosis root avulsion rather than a distal lesion.

Axillary Nerve (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Nervus axillaris · Nerve · Peripheral Nervous System

A terminal branch of the posterior cord that passes through the quadrangular space with the posterior circumflex humeral vessels, winding around the surgical neck of the humerus to supply deltoid and teres minor.

Roots
C5, C6 — from the posterior cord
Course
Through the quadrangular space, then around the surgical neck of the humerus
Motor
Deltoid and teres minor
Sensory
Superior lateral cutaneous nerve of the arm — the "regimental badge" area
Clinical relevance. The most commonly injured nerve in shoulder dislocation and surgical neck fracture. Always test regimental badge sensation and deltoid contraction before and after reduction. It also limits how far the deltoid-splitting lateral approach can extend — roughly 5 cm below the acromion.

Suprascapular Nerve (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Nervus suprascapularis · Nerve · Peripheral Nervous System

A branch of the upper trunk that passes through the suprascapular notch beneath the transverse scapular ligament to supply supraspinatus, then round the spinoglenoid notch to reach infraspinatus.

Roots
C5, C6 — from the upper trunk
Course
Through the suprascapular notch (under the ligament), then the spinoglenoid notch
Motor
Supraspinatus and infraspinatus
Sensory
Glenohumeral and acromioclavicular joints (no cutaneous branch)
Clinical relevance. Entrapment at the suprascapular notch weakens both muscles; entrapment at the spinoglenoid notch spares supraspinatus and affects infraspinatus alone — a pattern seen in volleyball players and with ganglion cysts. It also tethers a massively retracted cuff tear, limiting how far the tendon can be mobilised.

Long Thoracic Nerve (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Nervus thoracicus longus · Nerve · Peripheral Nervous System

Formed from the C5, C6 and C7 roots, it descends on the superficial surface of serratus anterior along the medial wall of the axilla — a long, superficial and therefore vulnerable course.

Roots
C5, C6, C7 (from the roots, before the trunks)
Course
Pierces scalenus medius, descends on the outer surface of serratus anterior
Motor
Serratus anterior only
Relations
Runs on the medial wall of the axilla, at risk in axillary dissection
Clinical relevance. Injury causes medial winging of the scapula and loss of overhead elevation. Classic causes are axillary node dissection, chest tube placement, backpack straps and viral brachial neuritis. Most traction injuries recover spontaneously over 12–24 months.

Dorsal Scapular Nerve (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Nervus dorsalis scapulae · Nerve · Peripheral Nervous System

A branch straight off the C5 root that pierces scalenus medius and runs deep to levator scapulae to supply the rhomboids.

Roots
C5 (directly from the root)
Course
Pierces scalenus medius, then deep to levator scapulae along the medial scapular border
Motor
Rhomboid major and minor; often levator scapulae
Sensory
None
Clinical relevance. Injury produces a subtle lateral drift of the scapula with weak retraction — far less dramatic than trapezius or serratus palsy and frequently missed. It is a recognised, if uncommon, cause of chronic interscapular pain in overhead athletes.

Thoracodorsal Nerve (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Nervus thoracodorsalis · Nerve · Peripheral Nervous System

The middle subscapular nerve, from the posterior cord, running down the posterior axillary wall with the thoracodorsal vessels to enter latissimus dorsi.

Roots
C6, C7, C8 — posterior cord
Course
Down the posterior wall of the axilla on subscapularis, with the thoracodorsal artery
Motor
Latissimus dorsi
Sensory
None
Clinical relevance. It is at risk during axillary lymph node clearance, and its integrity is essential for the pedicled latissimus dorsi flap used in breast and chest-wall reconstruction. Loss weakens the pull-up and the push off from a chair, which matters greatly to wheelchair users.

Musculocutaneous Nerve (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Nervus musculocutaneus · Nerve · Peripheral Nervous System

Arises from the lateral cord, pierces coracobrachialis and runs between biceps and brachialis, ending as the lateral cutaneous nerve of the forearm.

Roots
C5, C6, C7 — from the lateral cord
Motor
Coracobrachialis, biceps brachii, brachialis
Sensory
Lateral cutaneous nerve of the forearm
Course
Pierces coracobrachialis, then lies between biceps and brachialis
Clinical relevance. Isolated injury is uncommon but occurs with anterior shoulder dislocation and in the deltopectoral approach if retraction near the coracoid is too vigorous — the nerve enters coracobrachialis about 5–8 cm below the coracoid, defining a "safe zone".

Medial & Lateral Pectoral Nerves (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Nn. pectorales medialis et lateralis · Nerve · Peripheral Nervous System

Branches of the lateral and medial cords supplying the pectoral muscles. Confusingly, the *lateral* pectoral nerve supplies only pectoralis major, while the *medial* nerve supplies both pectoralis minor and the lower part of major.

Roots
Lateral — C5–C7 (lateral cord); Medial — C8, T1 (medial cord)
Course
Lateral pierces the clavipectoral fascia; medial pierces pectoralis minor
Motor
Pectoralis major and minor
Naming
Named for the cord of origin, not for their position on the chest
Clinical relevance. They must be identified and preserved when raising a pectoralis major flap; dividing them denervates the flap and it atrophies. Both are also encountered in the deltopectoral approach to the shoulder.

Lymphatics

Axillary Lymph Nodes (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Nodi lymphoidei axillares · Lymphatics · Immune System

A group of 20–40 nodes in the axilla, arranged in five groups (anterior, posterior, lateral, central and apical). They receive lymph from the upper limb, the chest wall and — critically — about 75% of the breast.

Groups
Anterior (pectoral), posterior (subscapular), lateral (humeral), central and apical
Afferents
Upper limb, chest wall, most of the breast
Efferents
Subclavian lymph trunk → thoracic duct (left) or right lymphatic duct
Levels
Surgically classified relative to pectoralis minor — I (lateral), II (behind), III (medial)
Clinical relevance. Axillary node status is the single most important prognostic factor in breast cancer. Extensive dissection risks lymphoedema of the arm and injury to the long thoracic nerve (winged scapula) and thoracodorsal nerve — which is why sentinel node biopsy has largely replaced routine clearance.
