Thigh & Knee Anatomy

Bones, muscles, ligaments, vessels and nerves — with origins, insertions, nerve supply, actions and the clinical points that matter, linked to a 3D model you can rotate.

Dr. Maninder SinghMS Orthopedics | Associate Professor, GMCH Amritsar
November 2026 9 min read

The knee is a hinge with a twist: the femoral condyles roll and glide on the tibial plateau, guided by the cruciate and collateral ligaments and cushioned by the menisci. The quadriceps extends the knee through the patella and patellar ligament, while the hamstrings flex it. The popliteal fossa behind the knee contains the popliteal artery, vein and tibial and common peroneal nerves — structures at risk in knee dislocations and posterior surgery.

Bones

Femur (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Os femoris · Bone · Lower Limb

The longest, heaviest and strongest bone in the body, comprising about a quarter of standing height. Its head sits deep in the acetabulum; the shaft is angled medially (the neck-shaft angle averages 125°) so the knees sit under the body's centre of gravity. The femoral head's blood supply — mainly retrograde, from the medial circumflex femoral artery — governs the entire management of hip fractures.

Articulations

  • Hip joint (acetabulum)
  • Knee joint (tibia and patella)

Muscle & ligament attachments

  • Iliopsoas (lesser trochanter)
  • Gluteus medius and minimus (greater trochanter)
  • Quadriceps (vastus origins along the shaft)
  • Adductor group (linea aspera)
  • Gastrocnemius heads (posterior condyles)

Key landmarks

  • Femoral head — Two-thirds of a sphere, covered in cartilage except at the fovea where the ligamentum teres attaches. Blood supply is retrograde — displaced neck fractures threaten it directly.
  • Femoral neck — The commonest fragility fracture site in the elderly. Neck-shaft angle averages 125°; anteversion averages 15°.
  • Greater trochanter — Palpable lateral prominence and insertion of the hip abductors (gluteus medius and minimus) — the key to a level pelvis when walking.
  • Lesser trochanter — Posteromedial prominence; insertion of iliopsoas, the chief hip flexor.
  • Linea aspera — Rough posterior ridge along the shaft — attachment for adductors, vastus muscles and the short head of biceps femoris; a landmark for rotational alignment during nailing.
  • Medial condyle — Larger than the lateral; carries the adductor tubercle for adductor magnus and the origin of the medial collateral ligament.
  • Lateral condyle — Origin of the lateral collateral ligament and popliteus; forms the lateral wall of the intercondylar notch where the cruciate ligaments run.
  • Intercondylar notch — Houses the ACL and PCL. A narrow notch is a recognised risk factor for ACL rupture.
Neck of femur fracture. In the elderly with a displaced fracture, the head's blood supply is unreliable — replacement (hemiarthroplasty or THR) outperforms fixation. Surgery within 48 hours, plus osteoporosis treatment on discharge, reduces mortality.
Femoral shaft fracture. Can lose 1–1.5 litres of blood into the thigh. Closed intramedullary nailing within 24 hours in a stable patient gives union rates above 95% and lowers pulmonary complications.
Avascular necrosis. Steroids, alcohol, sickle cell disease and trauma are the leading causes. Once the head collapses (Ficat III), joint replacement outperforms every head-preserving procedure.

Patella (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Patella · Bone · Lower Limb

The largest sesamoid bone in the body, embedded within the quadriceps tendon. It increases the moment arm of the extensor mechanism by 30–50%, making knee extension dramatically more efficient, and protects the front of the knee. Its posterior surface carries the thickest articular cartilage in the body — up to 6 mm.

Articulations

  • Patellofemoral joint (trochlear groove of femur)

Muscle & ligament attachments

  • Quadriceps tendon (base)
  • Patellar tendon (apex)
  • Medial and lateral retinacula

Key landmarks

  • Base (superior pole) — Receives the quadriceps tendon insertion.
  • Apex (inferior pole) — Origin of the patellar tendon running to the tibial tuberosity; the site of "jumper's knee" (patellar tendinopathy).
  • Median ridge — Divides medial and lateral facets and tracks in the femoral trochlear groove.
  • Medial patellofemoral ligament — The principal restraint to lateral patellar dislocation — almost always torn when the patella dislocates.
Patella fracture. Inability to perform a straight-leg raise indicates a disrupted extensor mechanism and mandates surgery. Tension-band wiring converts quadriceps pull into compression, so early movement actively aids healing.
Patellar dislocation. Almost always lateral, most often in adolescent girls and in those with trochlear dysplasia or patella alta. First-time dislocations are usually managed conservatively with bracing and vastus medialis rehabilitation.

Muscles

Quadriceps Femoris (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Musculus quadriceps femoris · Muscle · Musculoskeletal

The great extensor group of the thigh: rectus femoris (which also flexes the hip, crossing two joints) plus the three vasti — lateralis, medialis and intermedius. All converge into the quadriceps tendon, the patella and the patellar tendon.

Origin
Rectus femoris — anterior inferior iliac spine; vasti — femoral shaft
Insertion
Tibial tuberosity via the patella and patellar tendon
Nerve
Femoral nerve (L2, L3, L4)
Action
Knee extension; rectus femoris also flexes the hip
Blood supply
Femoral artery, profunda femoris, lateral circumflex femoral and descending genicular arteries.
Venous drainage
Venae comitantes to the femoral vein.
Lymphatic drainage
Deep inguinal nodes; rectus femoris also to superficial inguinal.
Clinical relevance. Quadriceps strength is the single best predictor of function after knee injury or replacement — and vastus medialis obliquus wastes within days of a knee injury. Rehabilitation begins with static quadriceps work on day one.

Hamstrings (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Musculi ischiocrurales · Muscle · Musculoskeletal

Three muscles — biceps femoris, semitendinosus and semimembranosus — spanning hip and knee. They extend the hip and flex the knee, and act as important dynamic protectors of the ACL by resisting anterior tibial translation.

Origin
Ischial tuberosity (short head of biceps femoris from the linea aspera)
Insertion
Head of the fibula (biceps femoris); medial tibia (semitendinosus, semimembranosus)
Nerve
Sciatic nerve — tibial division (short head of biceps: common peroneal division)
Action
Hip extension and knee flexion
Blood supply
Perforating branches of the profunda femoris, with the inferior gluteal proximally and popliteal distally.
Venous drainage
To the profunda femoris and femoral veins.
Lymphatic drainage
Deep inguinal and gluteal nodes to the internal iliac.
Clinical relevance. Hamstring strains are the commonest injury in sprinting sports, with high recurrence when rehabilitation is rushed. Eccentric strengthening (Nordic curls) is the best-evidenced prevention. The semitendinosus and gracilis tendons are the standard autograft for ACL reconstruction.

Sartorius (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Musculus sartorius · Muscle · Musculoskeletal

The longest muscle in the body, ribboning obliquely across the thigh from the ASIS to the medial tibia — the "tailor's muscle" that produces the cross-legged sitting position.

Origin
Anterior superior iliac spine
Insertion
Medial proximal tibia as part of the pes anserinus
Nerve
Femoral nerve (L2, L3)
Action
Hip flexion, abduction and external rotation; knee flexion
Blood supply
Muscular branches of the femoral artery, lateral circumflex femoral and descending genicular.
Venous drainage
To the femoral vein.
Lymphatic drainage
Superficial and deep inguinal nodes.
Clinical relevance. It forms the lateral border of the femoral triangle and the roof of the adductor canal. Its tendon, with gracilis and semitendinosus, makes up the pes anserinus — a common site of bursitis causing medial knee pain that is often mistaken for meniscal pathology.

Popliteus (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Musculus popliteus · Muscle · Musculoskeletal

A small triangular muscle in the floor of the popliteal fossa, running from the lateral femoral condyle to the posterior tibia — uniquely, it passes inside the knee capsule.

Origin
Lateral femoral condyle (intracapsular) and lateral meniscus
Insertion
Posterior surface of the proximal tibia, above the soleal line
Nerve
Tibial nerve (L4–S1)
Action
Unlocks the fully extended knee by laterally rotating the femur on the tibia; retracts the lateral meniscus
Blood supply
Genicular branches of the popliteal and the posterior tibial recurrent artery.
Venous drainage
To the popliteal vein.
Lymphatic drainage
Popliteal nodes.
Clinical relevance. Popliteus "unlocks" the screw-home mechanism, so it initiates every step out of full extension. It is a component of the posterolateral corner — injury there causes rotatory instability and, if unrecognised, is a leading cause of failed cruciate ligament reconstruction.

Plantaris (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Musculus plantaris · Muscle · Musculoskeletal

A small muscle with a very long slender tendon running between gastrocnemius and soleus to the calcaneus. It is absent in roughly 10% of people and is functionally almost negligible.

Origin
Lateral supracondylar line of the femur
Insertion
Calcaneus, medial to the Achilles tendon
Nerve
Tibial nerve (S1, S2)
Action
Weak plantarflexion of the ankle and flexion of the knee
Blood supply
Lateral sural artery from the popliteal.
Venous drainage
To the popliteal vein.
Lymphatic drainage
Popliteal nodes.
Clinical relevance. Rupture causes "tennis leg" — a sudden painful snap in the calf that mimics an Achilles tear or a deep vein thrombosis, and needs ultrasound to distinguish. Its long tendon is a favoured donor graft for reconstructing hand and ankle tendons.

Tendons & ligaments

Quadriceps Tendon (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Tendo musculi quadricipitis femoris · Tendon · Connective Tissue

The confluence of the four heads of quadriceps femoris, inserting into the superior pole of the patella. With the patellar ligament it forms the extensor mechanism of the knee, the patella acting as a sesamoid that lengthens the lever arm.

Formation
Rectus femoris, vastus lateralis, medialis and intermedius
Insertion
Base (superior pole) of the patella
Function
Knee extension; the patella increases the moment arm of the quadriceps
Layers
Three laminae — rectus superficial, vasti middle, intermedius deep
Clinical relevance. Quadriceps tendon rupture typically occurs just above the patella in patients over 40, often with diabetes, gout, chronic renal failure or steroid use. A palpable suprapatellar gap and inability to perform a straight-leg raise are the key signs; early repair restores extensor continuity.

Patellar Ligament (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Ligamentum patellae · Tendon · Connective Tissue

The continuation of the extensor mechanism below the patella, running from the apex of the patella to the tibial tuberosity. It is functionally the tendon of insertion of quadriceps, the patella being an intercalated sesamoid bone.

Origin
Apex (inferior pole) of the patella
Insertion
Tibial tuberosity
Function
Transmits quadriceps force to the tibia for knee extension
Relations
Deep infrapatellar bursa lies between it and the tibia; infrapatellar fat pad lies behind it
Clinical relevance. It is the site of the patellar (knee-jerk) reflex, L3–L4, and the graft harvested in bone–patellar tendon–bone ACL reconstruction. Osgood–Schlatter disease is a traction apophysitis at its tibial insertion in adolescents; patellar tendinopathy ("jumper’s knee") affects its proximal fibres.

Arteries

Femoral & Popliteal Artery (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

A. femoralis et A. poplitea · Artery · Cardiovascular

The main artery of the lower limb. It enters the thigh under the inguinal ligament at the mid-inguinal point, passes down the adductor canal, and becomes the popliteal artery as it passes through the adductor hiatus into the back of the knee.

Origin
Continuation of the external iliac artery beneath the inguinal ligament
Course
Femoral triangle → adductor canal → adductor hiatus → popliteal fossa
Termination
Divides into anterior and posterior tibial arteries below the knee
Surface
Femoral pulse at the mid-inguinal point, midway between the ASIS and the pubic symphysis
Clinical relevance. The femoral artery is the standard access route for cardiac catheterisation and angiography. The popliteal artery is tethered at the adductor hiatus and the soleal arch, making it highly vulnerable in knee dislocation — a documented pulse check and often CT angiography are mandatory in any dislocated knee.

Veins

Femoral & Popliteal Veins (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Vena femoralis et poplitea · Vein · Cardiovascular

The deep venous drainage of the lower limb, accompanying the corresponding arteries. These are the veins in which the clinically important deep vein thromboses form, and from which pulmonary emboli originate.

Formation
Popliteal vein from the tibial veins; becomes femoral vein at the adductor hiatus
Course
Femoral vein lies medial to the artery in the femoral triangle
Termination
Becomes the external iliac vein at the inguinal ligament
Valves
Numerous one-way valves direct flow proximally, assisted by the calf muscle pump
Clinical relevance. Proximal DVT (popliteal and above) carries the highest risk of pulmonary embolism. After major lower-limb orthopaedic surgery, mechanical and chemical prophylaxis plus early mobilisation are standard — the calf muscle pump is the body's own protection, and immobility disables it.

Great Saphenous Vein (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Vena saphena magna · Vein · Cardiovascular

The longest vein in the body, running from the medial side of the foot, in front of the medial malleolus, up the medial leg and thigh to drain into the femoral vein at the saphenous opening in the groin.

Origin
Medial end of the dorsal venous arch of the foot
Course
Anterior to the medial malleolus, up the medial leg and thigh
Termination
Femoral vein at the saphenofemoral junction (about 4 cm below and lateral to the pubic tubercle)
Relations
Accompanied by the saphenous nerve below the knee
Clinical relevance. Its constant position anterior to the medial malleolus makes it the classic site for emergency venous cutdown in shock. It is also the standard conduit for coronary artery bypass grafting, and the vein most often treated in varicose vein surgery. The nearby saphenous nerve is at risk during harvest and in medial ankle approaches.

Nerves

Femoral Nerve (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Nervus femoralis · Nerve · Peripheral Nervous System

The largest branch of the lumbar plexus and the motor nerve of knee extension. It emerges lateral to psoas, passes under the inguinal ligament lateral to the femoral artery, and immediately divides into multiple branches.

Roots
L2, L3, L4 (posterior divisions)
Motor
Iliacus, sartorius, pectineus and the entire quadriceps femoris
Sensory
Anterior thigh; and via the saphenous nerve, the medial leg and foot
Course
Under the inguinal ligament, lateral to the femoral artery (mnemonic NAVY: Nerve, Artery, Vein, Y-fronts — lateral to medial)
Clinical relevance. Femoral nerve block gives excellent analgesia for femoral shaft and hip fractures, reducing opioid requirements substantially — but it weakens the quadriceps, so falls precautions are essential afterwards. Injury causes loss of knee extension and an absent knee jerk.

Saphenous Nerve (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Nervus saphenus · Nerve · Peripheral Nervous System

The largest and longest cutaneous branch of the femoral nerve. It travels through the adductor canal, emerges on the medial side of the knee, and accompanies the great saphenous vein to the medial foot.

Origin
Femoral nerve, within the femoral triangle
Course
Adductor canal, then subcutaneous on the medial leg with the great saphenous vein
Sensory
Medial knee, medial leg and medial foot
Motor
None
Clinical relevance. Its infrapatellar branch is routinely cut in the midline knee approach, producing a patch of numbness lateral to the scar that patients should be warned about. The nerve is also at risk during great saphenous vein harvest, medial meniscal repair and tibial nailing.

Lymphatics

Popliteal Lymph Nodes (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Nodi lymphoidei poplitei · Lymphatics · Immune System

A small group of 6–7 nodes embedded in the fat of the popliteal fossa around the popliteal vessels, draining the lateral foot, the deep structures of the leg and the knee joint.

Location
Popliteal fossa, around the popliteal vessels
Afferents
Lateral foot and leg (via the small saphenous vein), knee joint, deep leg structures
Efferents
Deep inguinal nodes along the femoral vessels
Note
Most of the superficial lower limb bypasses these nodes and drains straight to the inguinal group
Clinical relevance. Enlargement here is easily missed because the nodes are deep in fat — palpate with the knee flexed to relax the fascia. Infection of the heel or lateral foot may enlarge popliteal rather than inguinal nodes, which can be a diagnostic clue.
