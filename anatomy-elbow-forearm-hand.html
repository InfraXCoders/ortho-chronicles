Elbow, Forearm, Wrist & Hand Anatomy

Bones, muscles, ligaments, vessels and nerves — with origins, insertions, nerve supply, actions and the clinical points that matter, linked to a 3D model you can rotate.

Dr. Maninder SinghMS Orthopedics | Associate Professor, GMCH Amritsar
November 2026 12 min read

From the elbow’s hinge and pivot to the eight carpal bones and a dozen nerves and tendons crossing the wrist, the forearm and hand pack extraordinary function into little space. The median, ulnar and radial nerves each supply a defined set of muscles and a patch of skin, so a focused examination of grip, pinch and sensation localises a nerve injury precisely. Knowing the carpal bones and the flexor retinaculum explains carpal tunnel syndrome, scaphoid fractures and distal radius injuries.

Bones

Radius (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Radius · Bone · Upper Limb

The lateral (thumb-side) forearm bone. It is narrow proximally and broad distally — the reverse of the ulna. The radius carries the hand: it rotates around the fixed ulna in pronation and supination, and roughly 80% of axial load across the wrist passes through the distal radius, which is why it is the most commonly fractured bone in adults.

Articulations

  • Proximal and distal radio-ulnar joints (rotation)
  • Radiocapitellar joint (elbow)
  • Radiocarpal joint (wrist)

Muscle & ligament attachments

  • Biceps brachii (radial tuberosity)
  • Supinator, pronator teres, pronator quadratus
  • Brachioradialis (distal radial styloid)

Key landmarks

  • Radial head — Disc-shaped, held by the annular ligament; rotates in the radial notch of the ulna. Fracture here is the commonest elbow fracture in adults.
  • Radial tuberosity — Insertion of the biceps tendon — the anatomical basis of biceps as the powerful supinator of the forearm.
  • Radial styloid — Distal lateral prominence, normally sitting about 1 cm more distal than the ulnar styloid — loss of this relationship indicates radial shortening after fracture.
  • Lister's tubercle — Dorsal ridge acting as a pulley for extensor pollicis longus (which may rupture after distal radius fracture); a key landmark for wrist arthroscopy portals.
  • Distal articular surface — Normally shows ~11° volar tilt and ~22° radial inclination — the reduction targets when fixing a Colles fracture.
Distal radius fracture. Check an X-ray at one week: comminuted fractures often re-displace in plaster. Restoring volar tilt and radial length — usually with a volar locking plate when unstable — protects long-term grip and rotation.
Essex-Lopresti injury. Radial head fracture with interosseous membrane disruption and distal radio-ulnar joint injury. Excising the radial head in this setting causes catastrophic proximal radial migration — always examine the wrist in a radial head fracture.

Ulna (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Ulna · Bone · Upper Limb

The medial forearm bone and the stable strut of the forearm. Broad proximally, where the trochlear notch grips the humeral trochlea to form the hinge of the elbow, and narrow distally, where it plays little part in the wrist joint. The ulna stays fixed while the radius rotates around it during pronation and supination.

Articulations

  • Humero-ulnar joint (elbow hinge)
  • Proximal and distal radio-ulnar joints

Muscle & ligament attachments

  • Triceps brachii (olecranon)
  • Brachialis (coronoid)
  • Flexor and extensor carpi ulnaris
  • Pronator quadratus

Key landmarks

  • Olecranon — The subcutaneous point of the elbow and insertion of triceps. A displaced fracture disables active elbow extension and needs tension-band fixation.
  • Trochlear notch — The C-shaped articular surface that grips the humeral trochlea, giving the elbow its stability as a hinge.
  • Coronoid process — Anterior buttress preventing posterior dislocation. Coronoid + radial head fracture + dislocation forms the "terrible triad" of the elbow.
  • Ulnar styloid — Distal medial prominence and anchor of the triangular fibrocartilage complex (TFCC), the main stabiliser of the distal radio-ulnar joint.
  • Subcutaneous border — Palpable along its whole length — the surgical approach and the reference for judging rotation during plating.
Monteggia fracture-dislocation. Ulnar shaft fracture with radial head dislocation. The dislocation is missed in up to a third of cases — always image the joint above and below any forearm fracture.
Nightstick fracture. Isolated ulnar shaft fracture from a direct blow while shielding the head. Minimally displaced injuries can be braced; displacement over 50% or angulation over 10° needs plating.

Scaphoid (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Os scaphoideum · Bone · Carpus

The largest bone of the proximal carpal row, bridging both rows and so acting as the mechanical link of the wrist. Its blood supply enters distally and runs retrograde, which is the single most important fact about it.

Articulations

  • Intercarpal joints of the wrist
Clinical relevance. The most commonly fractured carpal bone, typically from a fall on the outstretched hand. A normal initial X-ray does not exclude it — snuffbox tenderness is treated as a fracture with a cast and repeat imaging or MRI at 10–14 days. Proximal pole fractures risk avascular necrosis and non-union.

Lunate (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Os lunatum · Bone · Carpus

Crescent-shaped keystone of the proximal row, articulating with the distal radius and forming the floor of the carpal tunnel.

Articulations

  • Intercarpal joints of the wrist
Clinical relevance. The carpal bone most likely to dislocate; a perilunate/lunate dislocation is easily missed on a poor lateral X-ray. Avascular necrosis of the lunate is Kienböck disease, presenting as progressive wrist pain and stiffness.

Triquetrum (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Os triquetrum · Bone · Carpus

Pyramidal bone on the ulnar side of the proximal row, articulating with the hamate and, through the triangular fibrocartilage complex, with the ulna.

Articulations

  • Intercarpal joints of the wrist
Clinical relevance. The second most commonly fractured carpal, usually a dorsal chip from forced extension. Ulnar-sided wrist pain here overlaps with TFCC injury.

Pisiform (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Os pisiforme · Bone · Carpus

A pea-shaped sesamoid within the flexor carpi ulnaris tendon, sitting on the palmar surface of the triquetrum.

Articulations

  • Intercarpal joints of the wrist
Clinical relevance. Forms the ulnar wall of Guyon canal — the ulnar nerve and artery pass immediately lateral to it, so fracture or excision here risks ulnar nerve symptoms.

Trapezium (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Os trapezium · Bone · Carpus

Radial-most bone of the distal row, carrying the saddle-shaped first carpometacarpal joint that gives the human thumb its opposition.

Articulations

  • Intercarpal joints of the wrist
Clinical relevance. The first CMC joint is among the most common sites of symptomatic osteoarthritis in the hand, especially in women — presenting with pain at the base of the thumb on pinch and grip.

Trapezoid (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Os trapezoideum · Bone · Carpus

The smallest bone of the distal row, wedged securely between the trapezium, capitate and second metacarpal.

Articulations

  • Intercarpal joints of the wrist
Clinical relevance. Rarely injured precisely because it is so well protected — an isolated trapezoid fracture implies very high energy and warrants a search for other carpal injuries.

Capitate (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Os capitatum · Bone · Carpus

The largest carpal bone and the central pillar of the wrist, sitting at the axis around which carpal motion occurs.

Articulations

  • Intercarpal joints of the wrist
Clinical relevance. Its proximal pole also has a retrograde blood supply and can undergo avascular necrosis. Fracture usually accompanies a wider perilunate injury rather than occurring alone.

Hamate (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Os hamatum · Bone · Carpus

Wedge-shaped ulnar bone of the distal row, distinguished by its palmar hook (hamulus) projecting into the palm.

Articulations

  • Intercarpal joints of the wrist
Clinical relevance. Hook of hamate fracture is classic in golf, tennis and cricket from the butt of the handle. It causes ulnar-sided palm pain, is invisible on standard views (needs a carpal tunnel view or CT), and can rupture the little-finger flexor tendons.

Hand & Wrist (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Ossa manus · Bone · Upper Limb

Twenty-seven bones per hand: 8 carpals in two rows, 5 metacarpals and 14 phalanges. This dense articulation, driven by 34 muscles, produces the precision and power grips unique to the human hand. The carpal tunnel — floored by the carpal arch and roofed by the flexor retinaculum — transmits the median nerve and nine flexor tendons.

Articulations

  • Radiocarpal (wrist) joint
  • Intercarpal and carpometacarpal joints
  • Metacarpophalangeal and interphalangeal joints

Muscle & ligament attachments

  • Flexor and extensor tendon insertions
  • Thenar and hypothenar intrinsic muscles
  • Interossei and lumbricals

Key landmarks

  • Scaphoid — The most fractured carpal bone, injured by a fall on the outstretched hand. Its retrograde blood supply means proximal pole fractures risk avascular necrosis — tenderness in the anatomical snuffbox warrants a scaphoid series and often repeat imaging or MRI.
  • Lunate — The keystone of the proximal carpal row; the commonest carpal to dislocate and the site of Kienböck disease (avascular necrosis).
  • Hook of hamate — Palpable in the hypothenar eminence; fractured by racquet and club sports and closely related to the ulnar nerve in Guyon canal.
  • Carpal tunnel — Contains the median nerve and nine flexor tendons. Compression here causes carpal tunnel syndrome — night tingling in the thumb, index and middle fingers.
  • Anatomical snuffbox — Bounded by the EPL tendon and the APL/EPB tendons, floored by the scaphoid; the radial artery crosses here.
  • First carpometacarpal joint — The saddle joint that gives the thumb its opposition — and the joint most commonly affected by disabling osteoarthritis in the hand.
Scaphoid fracture. A normal initial X-ray does not exclude it. Snuffbox tenderness is treated in a scaphoid cast with re-imaging at 10–14 days or early MRI — missed fractures progress to non-union and wrist arthritis.
Carpal tunnel syndrome. Night tingling responds to splints and injection; thenar wasting and weakness mean the nerve is failing and warrant surgical release without delay.

Muscles

Brachioradialis (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Musculus brachioradialis · Muscle · Musculoskeletal

A long superficial forearm muscle running from the lateral supracondylar ridge to the radial styloid — the "beer-raising muscle", most efficient with the forearm in mid-pronation.

Origin
Lateral supracondylar ridge of the humerus
Insertion
Base of the radial styloid process
Nerve
Radial nerve (C5, C6) — unusual for a flexor
Action
Elbow flexion, strongest in the neutral (thumb-up) position
Blood supply
Radial recurrent artery (from the radial artery).
Venous drainage
Venae comitantes to the brachial and cephalic veins.
Lymphatic drainage
Cubital and lateral axillary nodes.
Clinical relevance. It is the anatomical guide to the radial nerve in the forearm and forms the lateral border of the cubital fossa. Its pull is a deforming force in distal radius fractures, drawing the fragment proximally and into radial deviation.

Common Flexor Group (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Compartimentum antebrachii flexorium · Muscle · Musculoskeletal

The anterior forearm compartment — pronator teres, the wrist and finger flexors and the deep flexor layer — arising largely from the common flexor origin on the medial epicondyle.

Origin
Medial epicondyle (common flexor origin), radius, ulna and interosseous membrane
Insertion
Carpal bones, metacarpals and the flexor tendons of the digits
Nerve
Median nerve, except FCU and the ulnar half of FDP (ulnar nerve)
Action
Wrist and finger flexion; forearm pronation
Blood supply
Ulnar and radial arteries with their recurrent and common interosseous branches.
Venous drainage
Venae comitantes to the brachial veins.
Lymphatic drainage
Cubital (supratrochlear) and axillary nodes.
Clinical relevance. Medial epicondylitis ("golfer's elbow") is degeneration at this common origin. The compartment is also where Volkmann ischaemic contracture develops after untreated compartment syndrome, classically following a supracondylar fracture in a child.

Common Extensor Group (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Compartimentum antebrachii extensorium · Muscle · Musculoskeletal

The posterior forearm compartment — the wrist and finger extensors plus supinator and the thumb outcropping muscles — largely from the common extensor origin on the lateral epicondyle.

Origin
Lateral epicondyle (common extensor origin), radius, ulna and interosseous membrane
Insertion
Metacarpal bases and the extensor expansions of the digits
Nerve
Radial nerve and its posterior interosseous branch (C7, C8)
Action
Wrist and finger extension; forearm supination
Blood supply
Radial, posterior interosseous and anterior interosseous arteries.
Venous drainage
Venae comitantes of the forearm.
Lymphatic drainage
Cubital and lateral axillary nodes.
Clinical relevance. Lateral epicondylitis ("tennis elbow") is a degenerative tendinopathy of the extensor carpi radialis brevis origin, not inflammation — which is why eccentric loading beats repeated steroid injection. The posterior interosseous nerve can be entrapped as it passes through supinator.

Tendons & ligaments

Distal Biceps Tendon (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Tendo musculi bicipitis brachii · Tendon · Connective Tissue

The tendon of biceps brachii descending across the cubital fossa to insert into the radial tuberosity, with the bicipital aponeurosis fanning off medially into the forearm fascia.

Insertion
Radial tuberosity
Aponeurosis
Bicipital aponeurosis (lacertus fibrosus) blends with forearm fascia over the brachial artery
Function
Powerful forearm supinator; secondary elbow flexor
Relations
Lies lateral to the brachial artery and median nerve in the cubital fossa
Clinical relevance. Distal biceps rupture presents with a proximally retracted (“reverse Popeye”) muscle belly and loss of supination power. A positive hook test — inability to hook a finger under the tendon — confirms it, and anatomical repair to the radial tuberosity restores supination strength.

Triceps Tendon (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Tendo musculi tricipitis brachii · Tendon · Connective Tissue

The common tendon of the three heads of triceps brachii, inserting into the olecranon process of the ulna. It is the principal extensor of the elbow.

Formation
Long, lateral and medial heads of triceps brachii
Insertion
Posterior aspect of the olecranon
Function
Elbow extension; the long head also aids shoulder extension and adduction
Relations
Lies subcutaneously over the posterior elbow
Clinical relevance. Triceps tendon rupture is rare and usually avulses a flake of olecranon; it presents with a palpable gap and weak extension against gravity. It is associated with anabolic steroid use, hyperparathyroidism and local steroid injection.

Interosseous Membrane of Forearm (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Membrana interossea antebrachii · Tendon · Connective Tissue

A fibrous sheet binding the radius to the ulna along their interosseous borders. Its central band runs obliquely from the radius down to the ulna, transferring axial load from the wrist to the elbow.

Attachments
Interosseous borders of the radius and ulna
Fibre direction
Central band runs distally from radius to ulna
Function
Transfers load from radius to ulna; stabilises the forearm; separates flexor and extensor compartments
Note
About 80% of axial load enters at the wrist through the radius
Clinical relevance. Its rupture is the missing link in the Essex-Lopresti injury — radial head fracture with distal radio-ulnar joint disruption. Excising the radial head when this membrane is torn allows catastrophic proximal migration of the radius, so the wrist must always be examined in a radial head fracture.

Flexor Retinaculum of Wrist (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Retinaculum musculorum flexorum · Tendon · Connective Tissue

The transverse carpal ligament — a thick fibrous band bridging the carpal arch from the scaphoid and trapezium laterally to the pisiform and hook of hamate medially, converting the arch into the carpal tunnel.

Attachments
Scaphoid tubercle and trapezium; pisiform and hook of hamate
Roofs
The carpal tunnel — median nerve and nine flexor tendons
Superficial to it
Ulnar nerve and artery in Guyon canal; palmar cutaneous branch of the median nerve
Function
Prevents bowstringing of the flexor tendons
Clinical relevance. This is the structure divided in carpal tunnel release. The palmar cutaneous branch of the median nerve runs superficial to it and is protected by keeping the incision ulnar to the palmaris longus axis. Guyon canal lies just superficial and ulnar, which is why ulnar symptoms can accompany carpal tunnel syndrome.

Extensor Retinaculum of Wrist (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Retinaculum musculorum extensorum · Tendon · Connective Tissue

A thickening of the deep fascia over the dorsum of the wrist that binds down the extensor tendons, sending septa to the radius to form six osseofibrous compartments.

Attachments
Distal radius laterally to the pisiform and triquetrum medially
Compartments
Six dorsal compartments transmitting the extensor tendons
Contents
First compartment — APL and EPB; sixth — extensor carpi ulnaris
Function
Prevents dorsal bowstringing of the extensor tendons
Clinical relevance. The first dorsal compartment is the site of De Quervain tenosynovitis (positive Finkelstein test); the third-compartment tendon, extensor pollicis longus, can rupture after a distal radius fracture as it frays around Lister tubercle.

Arteries

Radial & Ulnar Arteries (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

A. radialis et A. ulnaris · Artery · Cardiovascular

The paired arteries of the forearm arising from the brachial artery in the cubital fossa. They anastomose in the hand through the superficial and deep palmar arches, giving the hand a rich dual blood supply.

Origin
Brachial artery bifurcation in the cubital fossa
Course
Radial — lateral forearm, crossing the anatomical snuffbox; Ulnar — medial forearm, passing through Guyon canal with the ulnar nerve
Termination
Superficial (mainly ulnar) and deep (mainly radial) palmar arches
Surface
Radial pulse — lateral to the flexor carpi radialis tendon at the wrist
Clinical relevance. Allen test confirms a patent dual supply before radial artery harvest (for coronary bypass) or arterial line placement. The radial artery is also the standard access route for coronary angiography.

Nerves

Median Nerve (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Nervus medianus · Nerve · Peripheral Nervous System

The "labourer's nerve" — the principal nerve of forearm flexion and precision grip. It arises from the lateral and medial cords, runs down the arm without branching, and passes through the carpal tunnel into the hand.

Roots
C6–T1, from the lateral and medial cords
Motor
Most forearm flexors and pronators; thenar muscles and the lateral two lumbricals
Sensory
Palmar aspect of the thumb, index, middle and half the ring finger
Course
Medial to the brachial artery in the arm; between the heads of pronator teres; through the carpal tunnel
Clinical relevance. Carpal tunnel syndrome is the commonest entrapment neuropathy: night tingling in the radial three and a half digits, later thenar wasting and loss of thumb opposition. Splints and injection help early symptoms; weakness or wasting means the nerve is dying and warrants release. High lesions produce the "hand of benediction" on attempted fist closure.

Ulnar Nerve (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Nervus ulnaris · Nerve · Peripheral Nervous System

The nerve of fine finger control, arising from the medial cord. It passes behind the medial epicondyle in the cubital tunnel — the "funny bone" — then along the medial forearm into Guyon canal at the wrist.

Roots
C8–T1, from the medial cord
Motor
Flexor carpi ulnaris, medial half of flexor digitorum profundus, and almost all intrinsic hand muscles
Sensory
Little finger and the medial half of the ring finger, both surfaces
Course
Behind the medial epicondyle (cubital tunnel), then Guyon canal at the wrist
Clinical relevance. Cubital tunnel syndrome is the second commonest entrapment neuropathy. Damage causes clawing of the ring and little fingers and a positive Froment sign (thumb IP flexion when pinching). The "ulnar paradox": a higher lesion causes *less* clawing because the profundus to those fingers is also paralysed.

Radial Nerve (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Nervus radialis · Nerve · Peripheral Nervous System

The great extensor nerve of the upper limb and the largest branch of the brachial plexus, from the posterior cord. It spirals around the back of the humerus in the radial groove before winding forward to the front of the lateral epicondyle.

Roots
C5–T1, from the posterior cord
Motor
Triceps; brachioradialis; all wrist and finger extensors; supinator
Sensory
Posterior arm and forearm; dorsum of the lateral hand and first web space
Course
Radial (spiral) groove of the humerus → anterior to lateral epicondyle → divides into superficial and posterior interosseous branches
Clinical relevance. Wrist drop after a mid-shaft humeral fracture occurs in roughly 10–15% of cases; most are neurapraxias that recover in 3–4 months with a cock-up splint. "Saturday night palsy" follows prolonged compression of the nerve against the humerus. Test the first web space sensation and thumb extension.

Posterior Interosseous Nerve (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Nervus interosseus posterior · Nerve · Peripheral Nervous System

The deep motor branch of the radial nerve, passing between the two heads of supinator through the arcade of Frohse to supply the extensor compartment of the forearm.

Origin
Deep branch of the radial nerve at the elbow
Course
Between the superficial and deep heads of supinator (arcade of Frohse)
Motor
Extensor carpi ulnaris, finger and thumb extensors, abductor pollicis longus
Sensory
No cutaneous supply — wrist joint proprioception only
Clinical relevance. Palsy causes finger drop with *preserved* wrist extension (because ECRL is supplied more proximally) and no sensory loss — distinguishing it from a high radial nerve lesion. It is at direct risk during the anterior approach to the proximal radius and in radial head/neck fracture fixation, so forearm pronation is used to move it away.

Anterior Interosseous Nerve (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Nervus interosseus anterior · Nerve · Peripheral Nervous System

A purely motor branch of the median nerve arising just below the elbow and running down the interosseous membrane with the anterior interosseous artery.

Origin
Median nerve, about 5–8 cm distal to the lateral epicondyle
Motor
Flexor pollicis longus, the radial half of flexor digitorum profundus, pronator quadratus
Sensory
None — wrist joint proprioception only
Course
On the anterior interosseous membrane, deep to the long flexors
Clinical relevance. Anterior interosseous syndrome gives a pure motor palsy with no sensory loss: the patient cannot make an "OK" sign, pinching with a straight thumb and index tip instead. Because there is no numbness it is often dismissed as tendon rupture — nerve conduction studies settle it.

Lymphatics

Epitrochlear Lymph Nodes (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Nodi lymphoidei cubitales · Lymphatics · Immune System

One or two nodes lying superficial to the deep fascia about 4–5 cm above the medial epicondyle, alongside the basilic vein, draining the ulnar side of the hand and forearm.

Location
Above the medial epicondyle, along the basilic vein
Afferents
Ulnar side of the hand and forearm — little and ring fingers, medial forearm
Efferents
Lateral (humeral) group of axillary nodes
Palpation
Felt with the elbow flexed to about 90°
Clinical relevance. A palpable epitrochlear node is always significant in an adult. It suggests infection of the ulnar side of the hand, or systemic disease — it is a classic sign in secondary syphilis, and is also seen in lymphoma, sarcoidosis and infectious mononucleosis.
