Spine & Back Anatomy

Bones, muscles, ligaments, vessels and nerves — with origins, insertions, nerve supply, actions and the clinical points that matter, linked to a 3D model you can rotate.

Dr. Maninder SinghMS Orthopedics | Associate Professor, GMCH Amritsar
November 2026 8 min read

The spine protects the spinal cord while carrying the body’s weight and allowing movement. Its three curves — cervical and lumbar lordosis, thoracic kyphosis — spread load, and the discs and facet joints at each level control motion. The cord ends around L1–L2, below which the cauda equina nerve roots run in the canal, which is why a large lumbar disc can cause bladder and bowel symptoms. Deep muscles stabilise each segment while the erector spinae extend the whole column.

Bones

Cervical Spine (C1–C7)

Vertebrae cervicales · Bone · Vertebral Column

Seven cervical vertebrae form the most mobile region of the spine, carrying the head and housing the vertebral arteries within the transverse foramina (C6–C1). C1 (atlas) has no body and cradles the skull; C2 (axis) carries the odontoid peg around which the head rotates. The normal cervical lordosis is a gentle forward curve, straightened in muscle spasm.

Articulations

  • Atlanto-occipital (skull)
  • Atlanto-axial (rotation)
  • C2–C7 facet joints and intervertebral discs

Muscle & ligament attachments

  • Sternocleidomastoid, scalenes, levator scapulae
  • Trapezius via ligamentum nuchae
  • Longus colli — anterior stabiliser

Key landmarks

  • C1 — Atlas — Ring-shaped, bodyless vertebra articulating with the occipital condyles; provides ~50% of neck flexion-extension ("yes" joint).
  • C2 — Axis / odontoid — The dens (odontoid peg) is the pivot for ~50% of neck rotation ("no" joint). Type II odontoid fracture is the classic elderly neck injury.
  • C7 — Vertebra prominens — The most prominent spinous process at the neck base — the surface landmark for counting vertebral levels.
  • Transverse foramen — Canal transmitting the vertebral artery from C6 upward — vulnerable in cervical fractures and manipulation injuries.
  • Uncovertebral joints — Small lateral joints unique to the cervical spine; osteophytes here narrow the intervertebral foramen and cause radiculopathy.
Cervical myelopathy. Cord compression presents insidiously as clumsy hands, buttoning difficulty and unsteady gait — it is progressive and surgical. Any adult with these signs needs an MRI, not physiotherapy alone.
Whiplash. Most whiplash resolves with early movement and reassurance; collars and rest prolong recovery. Validated rules (Canadian C-Spine, NEXUS) determine who needs imaging.

Thoracic Spine (T1–T12)

Vertebrae thoracicae · Bone · Vertebral Column

Twelve thoracic vertebrae articulate with the ribs at costovertebral and costotransverse joints, forming the posterior wall of the thoracic cage. Their long, downward-sloping spinous processes overlap like roof tiles. The region is naturally kyphotic and comparatively rigid, which makes the thoracolumbar junction (T11–L2) a mechanical stress point and the commonest site of spinal fractures.

Articulations

  • Costovertebral and costotransverse joints (ribs)
  • Intervertebral discs and facet joints

Muscle & ligament attachments

  • Erector spinae group
  • Rhomboids and trapezius (via spinous processes)
  • Diaphragm crura (T12–L2)

Key landmarks

  • T1 vertebra — The transition to the cervical spine; T1 nerve root contributes to the brachial plexus and hand intrinsic function.
  • Costal facets — Paired demifacets on the vertebral bodies and transverse processes where each rib head articulates.
  • Spine of scapula level (T3) — Surface landmark: the scapular spine lies opposite the T3 spinous process.
  • Inferior scapular angle (T7) — Classic surface marker for the T7 level — used to count ribs and place chest drains safely.
  • Thoracolumbar junction (T12) — The hinge between a rigid kyphotic and a mobile lordotic spine — over half of all vertebral fractures occur at T12–L1.
Burst fracture & TLICS. At the thoracolumbar junction, management follows the TLICS score (fracture morphology + posterior ligamentous complex + neurology). A score ≥5 generally means surgical stabilisation.
Osteoporotic wedge fracture. Silent vertebral collapse causes progressive kyphosis and height loss. Losing more than 4 cm of height warrants a lateral spine X-ray and a DEXA scan.

Lumbar Spine (L1–L5)

Vertebrae lumbales · Bone · Vertebral Column

Five massive lumbar vertebrae carry the greatest compressive load in the spine. Their large kidney-shaped bodies, sagittally oriented facet joints and strong lordosis permit flexion and extension while limiting rotation. The spinal cord ends at about L1–L2 as the conus medullaris; below this the canal contains only the cauda equina nerve roots — which is why lumbar punctures are performed below L2.

Articulations

  • L5–S1 lumbosacral junction
  • Facet joints (sagittally oriented — limit rotation)
  • Intervertebral discs

Muscle & ligament attachments

  • Psoas major (all lumbar bodies and TPs)
  • Quadratus lumborum
  • Erector spinae and multifidus

Key landmarks

  • L1 — conus medullaris — The spinal cord terminates here in adults. Below this level the canal holds the cauda equina, permitting safe lumbar puncture at L3–L4 or L4–L5.
  • L3 transverse process — The longest lumbar transverse process; psoas major and quadratus lumborum attach here.
  • L4–L5 disc — With L5–S1, the most frequently herniated disc — producing L5 or S1 sciatica down the leg.
  • Iliac crest plane (L4) — Tuffier's line: a line joining the iliac crests crosses the L4 spinous process — the bedside landmark for lumbar puncture and spinal anaesthesia.
  • Pars interarticularis — The bony bridge between superior and inferior facets; a stress fracture here (spondylolysis) is the classic cause of back pain in young athletes.
Cauda equina syndrome. Saddle anaesthesia, urinary retention or bowel incontinence with back pain is a surgical emergency — decompression within 24–48 hours determines lifelong bladder function. Never send such a patient home to "try physiotherapy".
MRI caution. Disc bulges appear on MRI in 30–50% of completely pain-free adults. Image findings must match the clinical syndrome before they justify surgery.

Sacrum & Coccyx

Os sacrum et Os coccygis · Bone · Pelvic Girdle

The sacrum is a triangular bone of five fused vertebrae forming the keystone of the pelvic ring. It transmits the entire weight of the upper body through the sacroiliac joints to the pelvis and lower limbs. Four pairs of sacral foramina transmit the sacral nerve roots that form the sciatic and pudendal nerves. The coccyx is a small vestigial tail of 3–5 fused segments.

Articulations

  • Sacroiliac joints (bilateral)
  • L5–S1 lumbosacral junction
  • Sacrococcygeal joint

Muscle & ligament attachments

  • Piriformis (anterior sacrum)
  • Gluteus maximus
  • Erector spinae aponeurosis
  • Sacrotuberous and sacrospinous ligaments

Key landmarks

  • Sacral promontory — The prominent anterior lip of S1 — the key obstetric landmark for measuring the pelvic inlet.
  • Sacroiliac joint — A strong, minimally mobile joint transmitting body weight; a common and often overlooked source of low back and buttock pain.
  • Sacral hiatus — The inferior opening of the sacral canal — used for caudal epidural anaesthesia, especially in children.
  • S1 foramen — Transmits the S1 nerve root, the workhorse of ankle plantarflexion and the ankle jerk reflex.
  • Coccyx — Attachment for the pelvic floor and anococcygeal ligament; injury causes coccydynia — pain on sitting.
Sacral insufficiency fracture. In osteoporosis, sacral fractures occur with little or no trauma and are frequently missed on plain films — MRI or CT is diagnostic in the elderly patient with unexplained buttock pain.
Pelvic ring stability. The posterior sacroiliac complex is the true stabiliser of the pelvis; disruption here makes the ring unstable and is life-threatening in high-energy trauma.

Muscles

Erector Spinae (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Musculi erector spinae · Muscle · Musculoskeletal

The long paraspinal column — iliocostalis, longissimus and spinalis — running from sacrum and iliac crest to ribs, transverse processes and skull. It is the principal extensor and postural guardian of the spine.

Origin
Sacrum, iliac crest, thoracolumbar fascia and lumbar spinous processes
Insertion
Ribs, transverse and spinous processes, mastoid process
Nerve
Posterior rami of the spinal nerves
Action
Spinal extension; unilaterally, lateral flexion; eccentric control of forward bending
Blood supply
Dorsal branches of the posterior intercostal, subcostal and lumbar arteries (deep cervical and occipital above).
Venous drainage
Corresponding segmental veins.
Lymphatic drainage
Deep para-vertebral to intercostal, lumbar and deep cervical nodes.
Clinical relevance. Protective spasm here accounts for much acute low back pain and the flattened lumbar lordosis seen in disc disease. Preserving the multifidus and its innervation during posterior spinal exposure reduces long-term back pain — the basis of muscle-sparing and minimally invasive approaches.

Semispinalis Group (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Musculi semispinales · Muscle · Musculoskeletal

The largest muscle mass of the posterior neck, part of the transversospinalis group, spanning about five segments from transverse processes to spinous processes and the occiput.

Origin
Transverse processes of the lower cervical and thoracic vertebrae
Insertion
Spinous processes 4–6 segments above; semispinalis capitis onto the occipital bone
Nerve
Posterior rami of the spinal nerves
Action
Extension and contralateral rotation of the head and vertebral column
Blood supply
Deep cervical, occipital and dorsal branches of the posterior intercostal arteries.
Venous drainage
Corresponding segmental veins.
Lymphatic drainage
Deep cervical nodes.
Clinical relevance. The greater occipital nerve pierces semispinalis capitis before supplying the back of the scalp — entrapment here is a recognised cause of occipital neuralgia. Preserving the semispinalis attachment to C2 during posterior cervical surgery measurably reduces post-operative kyphosis and axial neck pain.

Multifidus & Rotatores (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Musculi multifidi et rotatores · Muscle · Musculoskeletal

The deepest layer of the transversospinalis group, spanning only one to four segments from transverse process to spinous process. They generate little gross movement; their role is fine segmental control, and they are dense with proprioceptors.

Origin
Transverse processes, sacrum and posterior ilium
Insertion
Spinous processes one to four segments above
Nerve
Medial branches of the posterior rami
Action
Segmental stabilisation; small rotation and extension of individual vertebrae
Blood supply
Dorsal branches of the posterior intercostal, lumbar and lateral sacral arteries.
Venous drainage
Corresponding segmental veins.
Lymphatic drainage
Deep para-vertebral to intercostal, lumbar and sacral nodes.
Clinical relevance. Multifidus atrophies rapidly and selectively after a back injury and does not recover spontaneously — which is why targeted motor-control retraining, not general exercise, is the evidence-based treatment. The medial branch that supplies it also innervates the facet joint, the anatomical basis of medial branch blocks and radiofrequency denervation.

Splenius Capitis & Cervicis (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Musculi splenii · Muscle · Musculoskeletal

Strap-like muscles wrapping the deeper neck muscles like a bandage (splenion, Greek for bandage), running from the midline down-and-back up to the mastoid and cervical transverse processes.

Origin
Ligamentum nuchae and spinous processes of C7–T6
Insertion
Mastoid process and superior nuchal line (capitis); transverse processes of C1–C3 (cervicis)
Nerve
Posterior rami of the middle and lower cervical spinal nerves
Action
Bilaterally extend the head and neck; unilaterally rotate and side-bend to the same side
Blood supply
Muscular branches of the occipital and deep cervical (transverse cervical) arteries.
Venous drainage
To the deep cervical and vertebral veins.
Lymphatic drainage
Occipital and deep cervical nodes.
Clinical relevance. A frequent source of cervicogenic headache and of the trigger points that refer pain to the vertex and behind the eye. Chronic overload from forward-head posture at a screen is the commonest provocation seen in clinic.

Serratus Posterior (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Musculi serrati posteriores · Muscle · Musculoskeletal

Two thin respiratory sheets deep to the rhomboids and latissimus — serratus posterior superior elevating the upper ribs, inferior depressing the lower ribs.

Origin
Spinous processes C7–T3 (superior) and T11–L2 (inferior)
Insertion
Upper borders of ribs 2–5 (superior) and lower borders of ribs 9–12 (inferior)
Nerve
Intercostal nerves
Action
Accessory muscles of respiration — elevate and depress the ribs
Blood supply
Posterior intercostal arteries.
Venous drainage
Posterior intercostal veins.
Lymphatic drainage
Intercostal and para-aortic nodes.
Clinical relevance. Serratus posterior superior is a well-recognised source of deep, poorly localised interscapular pain that refers down the arm and is easily mistaken for cervical radiculopathy — a useful differential when imaging of the neck is unremarkable.

Suboccipital Muscles (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Musculi suboccipitales · Muscle · Musculoskeletal

Four small deep muscles — rectus capitis posterior major and minor, obliquus capitis superior and inferior — connecting the occiput, atlas and axis. They bound the suboccipital triangle and are exceptionally rich in muscle spindles, making them key proprioceptive sensors for head position.

Origin
Spinous process of C2 and posterior arch of C1
Insertion
Inferior nuchal line of the occiput and transverse process of C1
Nerve
Suboccipital nerve (dorsal ramus of C1)
Contents of triangle
Vertebral artery and the suboccipital nerve
Blood supply
Muscular branches of the vertebral artery, with the deep cervical and occipital arteries.
Venous drainage
Suboccipital venous plexus.
Lymphatic drainage
Occipital and deep cervical nodes.
Clinical relevance. The vertebral artery runs across the floor of the suboccipital triangle, which is why upper cervical manipulation carries a dissection risk. These muscles connect to the dura by a myodural bridge, and their spasm is strongly implicated in cervicogenic headache.

Deep Cervical Flexors (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Musculi longus colli et capitis · Muscle · Musculoskeletal

Longus colli and longus capitis lie directly on the front of the cervical vertebral bodies, with rectus capitis anterior and lateralis at the top. They are the deep stabilisers of the cervical spine, holding the lordosis against the pull of the heavier superficial muscles.

Origin
Anterior bodies and transverse processes of C1–T3
Insertion
Anterior arch of the atlas, basilar occiput, cervical transverse processes
Nerve
Ventral rami of C1–C6
Action
Cranio-cervical flexion; segmental stabilisation of the cervical spine
Blood supply
Ascending cervical, inferior thyroid and vertebral arteries.
Venous drainage
To the vertebral and inferior thyroid veins.
Lymphatic drainage
Retropharyngeal and deep cervical nodes.
Clinical relevance. These are the muscles exposed and retracted in the anterior cervical approach, and they lie immediately in front of the vertebral bodies used as the ACDF landmark. Weakness here, with dominance of the superficial flexors, is a well-documented feature of chronic neck pain and whiplash — deep flexor retraining is the evidence-based rehabilitation.

Nerves

Spinal Cord & Cauda Equina

Medulla spinalis et Cauda equina · Nerve · Peripheral Nervous System

The spinal cord runs from the foramen magnum to the conus medullaris at about L1–L2 in the adult, giving off 31 pairs of spinal nerves. Below the conus, the lumbar and sacral roots continue as the cauda equina — a bundle of free nerve roots floating in cerebrospinal fluid.

Extent
Foramen magnum to conus medullaris at L1–L2 in adults (L3 at birth)
Enlargements
Cervical (C4–T1) for the upper limb; lumbosacral (L2–S3) for the lower limb
Cauda equina
Lumbar and sacral roots below L2, surrounded by CSF within the thecal sac
Blood supply
One anterior and two posterior spinal arteries, reinforced by radicular arteries including the artery of Adamkiewicz
Clinical relevance. Because the cord ends at L1–L2, lumbar puncture is performed at L3–L4 or L4–L5, where only the mobile cauda equina roots lie. Cauda equina syndrome — saddle anaesthesia with bladder or bowel dysfunction — is a surgical emergency requiring decompression within 24–48 hours. Above the conus, compression causes upper motor neurone signs (myelopathy); below it, lower motor neurone signs.

Greater Occipital Nerve (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Nervus occipitalis major · Nerve · Peripheral Nervous System

The large sensory branch of the C2 dorsal ramus, curling around the inferior oblique muscle and piercing semispinalis capitis and trapezius to supply the back of the scalp up to the vertex.

Origin
Dorsal ramus of C2
Course
Around obliquus capitis inferior, piercing semispinalis capitis and the trapezius aponeurosis
Sensory
Posterior scalp from the nuchal line to the vertex
Motor
Semispinalis capitis
Clinical relevance. Entrapment where it pierces the muscles causes occipital neuralgia — sharp, shooting pain from the neck over the scalp, often misdiagnosed as migraine. Diagnostic greater occipital nerve block, about 2 cm lateral to the external occipital protuberance, is both a test and a treatment.

Intercostal Nerves (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Nervi intercostales · Nerve · Peripheral Nervous System

The ventral rami of T1–T11, running in the costal groove at the lower border of each rib within the neurovascular bundle, between the internal and innermost intercostal muscles.

Origin
Ventral rami of thoracic spinal nerves T1–T11
Course
In the costal groove on the *inferior* border of the rib, ordered vein–artery–nerve from above down
Motor
Intercostal muscles, and the abdominal wall for the lower nerves
Sensory
Thoracic and abdominal wall skin in a dermatomal pattern
Clinical relevance. The VAN arrangement in the subcostal groove is why chest drains and thoracentesis needles are always passed over the *upper* border of the rib below. Rib fracture pain suppresses ventilation and causes atelectasis and pneumonia, so intercostal or paravertebral blocks are a genuine therapeutic intervention, not just comfort.
