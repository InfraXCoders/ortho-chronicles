Tennis Elbow:
Causes, Exercises & Treatment

You do not need to play tennis to get it. Lateral epicondylitis is an overuse tendon problem that responds well to the right exercises.

What Is Tennis Elbow?

Tennis elbow, or lateral epicondylitis, is a degenerative problem of the tendon that attaches the wrist-extensor muscles to the bony bump on the outer side of the elbow. Repeated gripping, twisting or lifting overloads the tendon and its fibres become disorganised and painful — it is more a tendinosis (degeneration) than a simple inflammation.

Only a small share of patients are tennis players. It commonly affects people aged 35–55 who use tools, type, lift bags, play racquet sports, paint, cook with heavy utensils or use a mouse for long hours. Pain on the outer elbow spreading into the forearm, weak grip, pain when lifting a cup or shaking hands, and tenderness over the bony bump are typical.

Diagnosis

A doctor diagnoses tennis elbow by examination: pain when the wrist is extended against resistance and tenderness at the lateral epicondyle. Imaging is generally unnecessary. An X-ray may show calcification in long-standing cases, and ultrasound or MRI is used if the diagnosis is uncertain or if a nerve entrapment (radial tunnel syndrome) or elbow joint disorder is suspected.

The Healing Timeline

Most people recover fully, but tendons heal slowly: around 80–90% are better within 6–12 months, and many much sooner when loading is managed well. Pain that lingers beyond 3–6 months despite good treatment prompts a review of the diagnosis and options.

Treatment That Works

1

Relative rest and modify the task

Reduce or change the aggravating activity — use two hands for lifting, grip with the palm up, use bigger-handled tools, avoid repetitive twisting. Total rest is not necessary.

2

Progressive tendon-loading exercises

Slow, controlled wrist-extensor strengthening is the core treatment: eccentric wrist extension with a light weight (3 sets of 10–15, once a day), grip strengthening and forearm stretching. Increase load gradually over weeks.

3

Counter-force brace or wrist splint

A strap just below the elbow can reduce pain with activity; it is a helpful short-term aid, not a cure.

4

Short-term pain relief

Ice after activity, and topical anti-inflammatory gel. Oral tablets help only briefly.

5

Racquet and equipment check

In sport, a correct grip size, lower string tension and technique coaching (especially a one-handed backhand) reduce load.

Steroid injections relieve pain quickly but are linked with worse results at 6–12 months and a higher recurrence rate compared with exercise alone. Doctors increasingly reserve them for people who cannot do their exercises because of pain.

If It Persists

  • PRP injection — may help some people with persistent symptoms; evidence is mixed
  • Shockwave therapy — an option in chronic cases
  • Surgery — after 6–12 months of well-delivered conservative care, removal of the degenerated tendon portion (open or arthroscopic) gives good relief in the large majority of those who undergo it

See a Doctor If

  • The elbow is hot, red, swollen or you have fever
  • Pain follows a fall or injury (possible fracture)
  • Pain spreads with neck pain, numbness or tingling down the arm
  • There is no improvement after 6–8 weeks of proper rehabilitation

Common Questions

Can I keep working?

Usually yes, with changes: vary tasks, take breaks and avoid heavy gripping with the wrist bent back.

Is massage or ultrasound therapy helpful?

Evidence is limited. Exercise is the treatment with the strongest support.

How is golfer’s elbow different?

Golfer’s elbow (medial epicondylitis) affects the inner side of the elbow and the wrist flexors; the principles of treatment are similar.

Will it come back?

Recurrence is possible if the aggravating habits return. Maintaining forearm strength reduces the risk.
