Rotator Cuff Tear:
Symptoms, Treatment & Recovery

The rotator cuff is four small tendons that lift and steady your shoulder. Tears are common after 50, and many do not need surgery.

What Is the Rotator Cuff?

The rotator cuff is a group of four muscles — supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor and subscapularis — whose tendons wrap the head of the humerus like a cuff. They rotate the arm and, more importantly, keep the ball centred in the socket as the big muscles (deltoid, pectoralis) lift the arm. The supraspinatus, which runs under the bony roof of the shoulder (the acromion), is the tendon most often torn.

Tears come in two broad types. Degenerative tears develop gradually with age, repetitive overhead work and a poor blood supply to the tendon — they are found on scans in more than half of people over 60, many of whom have no pain. Traumatic tears follow a fall on the outstretched arm, a heavy lift or a dislocation, with sudden pain and weakness.

Symptoms

  • Dull, deep shoulder ache, worse at night and when lying on the affected side
  • Pain and weakness lifting the arm to the side or overhead (combing hair, reaching a shelf)
  • A painful arc between about 60° and 120° of lifting
  • Clicking or catching, and difficulty holding the arm up against resistance
  • After an injury: sudden pain, a tearing sensation and inability to lift the arm (“pseudo-paralysis”)

Unlike frozen shoulder, passive movement (someone else lifting your arm) is usually preserved, but active lifting is weak or painful.

How It Is Diagnosed

Examination uses specific tests (Jobe’s empty-can, drop-arm, lift-off, belly-press) to see which tendon is involved. An X-ray may show a high-riding humeral head or spurs under the acromion. Ultrasound is a quick, low-cost way to see a tear and its size; MRI shows the tear’s size, retraction and the quality of the muscle (fatty degeneration), which strongly influences whether repair is likely to succeed.

Treatment Without Surgery

Most partial tears and many small full-thickness tears in older or low-demand patients improve without an operation:

  • Relative rest from overhead work and heavy lifting for a few weeks
  • Physiotherapy to strengthen the remaining cuff and the muscles that control the shoulder blade
  • Anti-inflammatory tablets or gels for short periods, as advised
  • A subacromial corticosteroid injection can ease pain enough to allow rehabilitation; repeated injections can weaken the tendon
  • Sleep position: use a pillow in front and avoid lying on the sore side

A supervised programme of about 12 weeks gives satisfactory results in a large share of patients with degenerative tears; the tear does not close, but pain and function can improve substantially.

When Surgery Is Advised

  • A recent traumatic full-thickness tear in an active patient, especially under 65
  • Large or rapidly enlarging tears before the muscle degenerates
  • Persistent pain and weakness after about 3–6 months of good rehabilitation
  • Heavy overhead occupation or sport demands

The standard operation is arthroscopic rotator cuff repair: through small keyhole cuts, the torn tendon is brought back to its attachment on the humerus with suture anchors. Large irreparable tears may need tendon transfers, a superior capsular reconstruction or, in arthritic shoulders, reverse shoulder replacement.

Recovery After Repair

1

0–6 weeks

Sling to protect the repair. Gentle passive movement by the physiotherapist; hand, wrist and elbow movement allowed.

2

6–12 weeks

Sling off; start active-assisted then active movement. No lifting.

3

3–6 months

Progressive strengthening; return to light daily activities and desk work.

4

6–12 months

Return to heavy work and overhead sport when strength and control are adequate.

Full recovery takes 6 to 12 months. Rushing strengthening is the commonest reason repairs fail to heal; re-tear rates are higher with large tears, smoking and diabetes.

See a Doctor Promptly If

  • Sudden inability to lift the arm after an injury
  • Fever, redness or swelling after an injection or surgery
  • Night pain that is constant and unrelenting, or pain with weight loss
  • Neck pain with arm numbness or weakness
  • Shoulder pain with chest pressure or breathlessness — call emergency services

Common Questions

Can a rotator cuff tear heal by itself?

A full-thickness tear does not close on its own, but symptoms often improve with rehabilitation and many people live comfortably with a tear.

Does a tear always get worse?

Some do, particularly larger symptomatic tears; periodic review helps decide when repair is better.

Will an injection fix it?

It can reduce pain for weeks to months but does not repair the tendon.

How soon can I drive after repair?

Usually around 6–8 weeks, once out of the sling and able to control the car safely.
