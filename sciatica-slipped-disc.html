Sciatica & Slipped Disc:
When to Worry

Most sciatica improves within weeks without surgery — but a few warning signs mean you should not wait. Learn the difference.

What Is Sciatica?

Sciatica is not a diagnosis in itself — it describes pain that travels from the lower back or buttock down the back of the leg, sometimes below the knee into the foot, caused by irritation of one of the nerve roots that form the sciatic nerve. The commonest cause (about 90% of cases) is a lumbar disc prolapse (“slipped disc”), in which the soft centre of a disc pushes through its outer ring and presses on or inflames a nerve root. Other causes include narrowing of the spinal canal or nerve exit (stenosis), a bony spur, and occasionally a tumour or infection.

Typical features: leg pain that is often worse than the back pain, a sharp, burning or electric quality, pain on coughing or sneezing, tingling or numbness in a strip down the leg, and pain that eases on lying down or walking but worsens with prolonged sitting.

Why Do Discs Slip?

Discs lose water and elasticity with age, which makes the outer ring more prone to cracking. Heavy lifting with a rounded back, repeated bending, prolonged sitting, smoking, obesity and a sedentary lifestyle increase the risk; so does driving long distances with vibration. Disc prolapse is most common between 30 and 50 years. Importantly, many people have disc bulges on MRI with no symptoms at all — the scan must always be interpreted together with the clinical picture.

The Good News: Natural Course

Most sciatica from a disc prolapse improves naturally. Around 70–90% of people get substantially better within 6–12 weeks, because the prolapsed disc tends to shrink on its own and the inflammation settles. Staying gently active recovers faster than bed rest, which should be limited to a day or two.

Non-Surgical Treatment

  • Stay mobile: short, frequent walks; avoid long periods of sitting; change position every 30 minutes
  • Pain relief: paracetamol and short courses of anti-inflammatory tablets as advised; nerve-pain medicines may be added for severe leg pain
  • Physiotherapy: core and hip strengthening, hamstring and nerve-gliding exercises, posture and lifting training
  • Heat or cold packs for muscle spasm
  • Epidural steroid injection: considered for severe leg pain lasting more than 4–6 weeks; gives short-term relief and may help avoid surgery in some
  • Quit smoking and lose weight to protect the discs for the long term

When Is Surgery Needed?

Surgery is for a minority of patients:

  • Emergency — cauda equina syndrome (see below)
  • Progressive leg weakness, foot drop or worsening numbness
  • Severe, disabling leg pain that has not improved after 6–12 weeks of good non-surgical treatment
  • Spinal stenosis with walking limited by leg pain and failed conservative care

The usual operation is a microdiscectomy or endoscopic discectomy — a small cut through which the surgeon removes the prolapsed fragment compressing the nerve. Leg pain relief is typically rapid and results are good in about 85–90%. Recurrence of disc prolapse at the same level occurs in a small percentage (about 5–10%), which is why back-protecting habits matter.

Red Flags — Go to Emergency Immediately

  • Difficulty passing urine or loss of control of bladder or bowel, or the inability to feel when you pass urine
  • Numbness around the genitals, buttocks or inner thighs (saddle numbness)
  • Weakness in both legs, rapidly worsening weakness or a dragging foot
  • Back pain with fever, unexplained weight loss, a history of cancer, or after significant trauma
  • Severe constant night pain that does not change with position
Cauda equina syndrome is a surgical emergency. Delay beyond 24–48 hours can lead to permanent bladder, bowel and sexual dysfunction. Do not wait for an outpatient appointment.

Protecting Your Back

  • Lift with bent knees and a straight back, holding the load close to the body
  • Strengthen the core and glutes — plank, bridge and bird-dog exercises
  • Walk every day and avoid sitting for more than 30–45 minutes at a stretch
  • Adjust your chair and screen height; keep feet flat on the floor
  • Maintain a healthy weight and do not smoke

Common Questions

Is bed rest good for a slipped disc?

No. Rest for a day or two at most; staying gently active speeds recovery.

Do I need an MRI right away?

Not usually. An MRI is advised if there are red flags, if pain is severe or persistent beyond 6 weeks, or before an injection or surgery.

Will the disc go back in?

The disc does not return to place, but the prolapsed part often shrinks and the nerve calms down.

Can sciatica come back?

It can, particularly if the causes are not addressed. Exercise, weight control and good posture lower the risk.
