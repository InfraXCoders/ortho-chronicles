Meniscus Tear:
Symptoms, Repair & Recovery

The menisci are the knee’s shock absorbers. Not every tear needs an operation — the type, location and your age decide.

What Is the Meniscus?

Each knee has two C-shaped rubbery cartilage pads — the medial and lateral menisci — that sit between the thigh and shin bones. They absorb shock, spread load across the joint and add stability. Only the outer third (the “red zone”) has a good blood supply, so only tears in that area can reliably heal; the inner two-thirds (the “white zone”) cannot.

Two groups get tears: young active people, from a twisting injury (often together with an ACL tear), and people over 40, in whom the meniscus is already degenerating and tears with a trivial twist — getting up from a squat, for example.

Symptoms

  • Pain along the joint line, worse on twisting, squatting or deep bending
  • Swelling that appears the next day (more gradual than the swelling of an ACL tear)
  • Catching, clicking or a feeling that something is stuck
  • Locking — the knee will not fully straighten, a sign of a displaced “bucket-handle” tear
  • Feeling of the knee giving way

Diagnosis

The McMurray and Thessaly tests and joint-line tenderness suggest a tear. MRI shows the tear’s pattern, location, and any cartilage or ligament damage. Note that MRI finds meniscal tears in many people over 50 with no symptoms, so it should be interpreted with the examination — and a tear on a scan does not automatically mean surgery.

Treatment Options

Non-surgical (first choice for most degenerative tears)
  • Physiotherapy to strengthen quadriceps, hamstrings and hips
  • Activity modification and weight loss
  • Short-term anti-inflammatory medicines or gels
  • Steroid injection for a flare
  • Studies show exercise therapy performs as well as arthroscopy for most degenerative tears
Surgery (selected cases)
  • Meniscus repair: sutures a tear in the well-supplied outer zone; preferred in young patients and combined with ACL surgery
  • Partial meniscectomy: trims the unstable torn piece when repair is not possible
  • Surgery is mainly for locked knees, mechanical symptoms persisting after rehabilitation, and repairable acute tears in active patients

Removing meniscus tissue speeds up recovery but increases the risk of arthritis over time, which is why surgeons try to save every possible millimetre.

Recovery

  • Trim (meniscectomy): walking within days; light activity in 1–2 weeks; sport in about 4–6 weeks
  • Repair: protected weight-bearing and limited bending for 4–6 weeks (you may use crutches and a brace); gradual strengthening; sport after 4–6 months
  • Both: physiotherapy is key to regaining strength and preventing stiffness

See a Doctor Promptly If

  • The knee is locked and will not straighten
  • A hot, red, swollen knee with fever
  • Sudden swelling and instability after a twisting injury (possible ligament injury)
  • Calf swelling or pain after surgery or immobility

Common Questions

Can a meniscus tear heal on its own?

Small stable tears at the outer edge can; many degenerative tears do not heal but become painless with rehabilitation.

Is it safe to exercise with a meniscus tear?

Low-impact exercise is usually safe and helpful; avoid deep squats and twisting on a planted foot until the knee settles.

Do I need an MRI?

If locking, persistent pain or an injury suggests a significant tear, yes; a routine scan for mild pain may not change treatment.

Will I get arthritis?

Risk increases after meniscus injury, more so after removal of large amounts of tissue.
