Low Back Pain:
Safe Exercises & Self-Care

Staying active, not bed rest, is the proven treatment for most low back pain. Here is a step-by-step plan you can start today.

Understanding Non-Specific Low Back Pain

Around 8 in 10 people have low back pain at some point. In the vast majority (about 90%) no single dangerous cause is found — it is called non-specific low back pain and arises from muscles, ligaments, discs and the small facet joints. It usually improves within 2–6 weeks, although it can recur. The aim of treatment is to settle pain, keep you moving, and build a back that tolerates daily loads. Read our back pain guide for causes and red flags and the article on sciatica if pain travels down the leg.

The First Days

  • Keep moving gently — short walks several times a day. Bed rest beyond 1–2 days delays recovery
  • Heat packs often relax tight muscles; cold may help in the first day or two — use whichever feels better
  • Simple pain relief such as paracetamol, or a short course of anti-inflammatory tablets if your doctor says it is safe for you
  • Change positions often; avoid sitting for more than 30–45 minutes
  • Return to normal activities as soon as comfortable; fear of movement prolongs pain

Starter Exercises (After the Worst Pain Eases)

1

Walking

Begin with 10 minutes and build to 30. Walking is the best single “exercise” for back pain.

2

Knee-to-chest stretch

Lie on your back, hug one knee to the chest for 20 seconds, 3 times each side. Do not bounce.

3

Pelvic tilt and bridge

Lie with knees bent; flatten the back and tilt the pelvis for 5 seconds (10 times). Progress to lifting hips into a bridge, hold 5 seconds, 10 times.

4

Cat–camel

On hands and knees, slowly arch then round the back, 10 cycles, within comfort.

5

Bird-dog

On hands and knees, extend the opposite arm and leg while keeping the back still, hold 5 seconds, 8 times each side.

6

Side plank (knees bent)

Hold 15–20 seconds, 3 times each side. Builds the deep stabilising muscles.

Stop any exercise that sends pain down the leg or makes numbness worse, and check with a physiotherapist. Consistency matters more than intensity: about 15 minutes a day beats an occasional long session.

Everyday Habits

  • Sit with hips and knees at about 90°, feet flat, low back supported; set the monitor at eye level
  • Lift with bent knees and a straight back, holding the load close
  • Avoid prolonged bending forward over a sink or while doing floor work — squat or kneel
  • Sleep on your side with a pillow between the knees, or on your back with a pillow under the knees
  • Keep a healthy weight, do not smoke, and sleep well — all linked to chronic back pain

What a Doctor or Physio Can Add

  • Examination to rule out serious causes
  • Physiotherapy: manual therapy, graded exercise, ergonomic advice
  • Short courses of medicines; muscle relaxants and nerve-pain medicines have limited, specific roles
  • Imaging (X-ray/MRI) only if red flags or no improvement after 6 weeks — images often show age-related changes unrelated to pain
  • Injections or surgery are rarely needed and are for specific diagnoses

Red Flags — See a Doctor Urgently

  • Loss of bladder or bowel control, numbness around the genitals or buttocks
  • Weakness in a leg or foot drop; progressive numbness
  • Fever, unexplained weight loss, or a history of cancer or TB
  • Back pain after significant trauma, or in people with osteoporosis
  • Severe night pain unrelieved by position
  • Pain that is not improving after 6 weeks

Common Questions

Should I avoid exercise if my back hurts?

Avoid the activity that provokes sharp pain, but keep moving. Gentle exercise speeds recovery.

Is a back belt useful?

Short-term use may ease pain during activity but long-term use can weaken the core.

Is yoga good for back pain?

Gentle yoga can help, but avoid deep forward-bends or forceful twists during a flare.

Will an MRI find the cause?

Often not — many pain-free people have disc bulges and degeneration on MRI.
