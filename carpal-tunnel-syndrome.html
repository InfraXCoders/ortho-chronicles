Carpal Tunnel Syndrome:
Symptoms & Treatment

Waking at night shaking your hand to bring the feeling back is the classic sign. Carpal tunnel syndrome is common, treatable, and best managed early.

What Is Carpal Tunnel Syndrome?

The median nerve travels from the forearm into the hand through a narrow passage at the wrist called the carpal tunnel, along with nine tendons that bend the fingers. When the lining of these tendons swells, or the tunnel is narrowed, the nerve is squeezed. The result is numbness, tingling and pain in the thumb, index, middle and half of the ring finger — the little finger is spared because it is supplied by a different nerve.

Carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS) affects about 3–6% of adults and is most common between 40 and 60 years, more often in women. Pregnancy, diabetes, an underactive thyroid, rheumatoid arthritis, obesity, wrist fractures and repetitive wrist-intensive work (typing, hand tools, vibrating machinery) are linked with it. In many people no single cause is found.

Symptoms to Recognise

  • Tingling or “pins and needles” in the thumb, index and middle fingers
  • Night waking with a numb or burning hand, relieved by shaking or hanging the arm (“flick sign”)
  • Numbness while driving, holding a phone or a book, or using a keyboard
  • Dropping things, clumsiness with buttons, weak pinch
  • Later: wasting of the muscle at the base of the thumb — a sign of significant nerve damage
Do not wait if you notice thumb-muscle wasting or constant numbness. Long-standing compression can cause permanent nerve damage; earlier treatment gives better recovery.

How Doctors Confirm It

Diagnosis rests on your story and a hand examination: tapping over the nerve (Tinel’s sign), holding the wrist bent for a minute (Phalen’s test) and checking pinch strength and sensation. Nerve conduction studies measure how fast signals travel through the wrist and grade severity; they are most useful when the picture is unclear, when surgery is planned or when symptoms are severe. Blood tests for diabetes and thyroid disease are often advised, and neck X-rays may be needed if a pinched nerve at the neck is suspected as a cause or companion.

Treatment Without Surgery

First steps (mild/moderate)
  • Neutral wrist night splint worn every night for at least 6–12 weeks
  • Avoid prolonged wrist flexion or extension, vary tasks, take micro-breaks
  • Treat associated conditions such as diabetes, thyroid disease
  • Tendon- and nerve-gliding exercises taught by a hand therapist
Next step
  • A single corticosteroid injection into the carpal tunnel often gives relief for weeks to months
  • Short courses of tablets are less helpful than splints/injection
  • Pregnancy-related CTS usually settles after delivery — splints are the main treatment

Around half of people with mild or moderate symptoms improve with splinting, and injections work well for many, but symptoms often return in a proportion of patients, which is when surgery is considered.

Carpal Tunnel Release Surgery

Surgery is recommended for severe symptoms, constant numbness, muscle wasting, or symptoms that return after non-surgical treatment. In carpal tunnel release the surgeon divides the tight ligament roof of the tunnel (the transverse carpal ligament), giving the nerve room. It is a short day-care operation, usually under local anaesthetic, through a small cut in the palm or an endoscopic technique.

  • Night pain and tingling often disappear within days
  • Light use of the hand is encouraged straight away; stitches come out at about 2 weeks
  • Return to desk work is often possible in 1–2 weeks and heavy manual work in 4–6 weeks
  • Strength and sensation may continue to improve for several months, more slowly if the nerve was badly compressed
  • Risks are low but include scar tenderness, infection, stiffness and, rarely, nerve injury; complex regional pain syndrome is very rare

Prevention & Daily Habits

  • Keep wrists in a neutral line when typing; lower the keyboard so the forearms are level
  • Use a light grip on tools and phones; take breaks every 30–45 minutes
  • Stretch the forearms and fingers during the day
  • Maintain a healthy weight and keep blood sugar and thyroid levels controlled
  • Wear a splint at night as soon as night tingling begins — this is the single best habit

See a Doctor Promptly If

  • Numbness is constant rather than intermittent
  • The thumb is becoming weak or its muscle is shrinking
  • Both hands and the feet are tingling (possible neuropathy), or symptoms follow a wrist injury
  • Neck pain with arm weakness appears at the same time

Common Questions

Can carpal tunnel go away on its own?

Mild cases, especially those linked to pregnancy or a temporary overuse, may settle. Persistent symptoms usually need splinting, an injection or surgery.

Is a splint really necessary at night?

Yes — most compression happens when the wrist curls during sleep, so a night splint treats the cause of the waking numbness.

Will surgery fix it permanently?

Release surgery is successful in most patients; recurrence is uncommon. Recovery of strength and sensation depends on how long the nerve was compressed.

Does typing cause carpal tunnel?

Evidence that ordinary keyboard use alone causes CTS is weak, but poor wrist posture and long periods of repetitive wrist-intensive work can aggravate it.
