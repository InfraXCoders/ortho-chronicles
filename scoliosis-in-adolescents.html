Scoliosis in Teenagers:
Signs, Bracing & Surgery

Most curves in growing children are mild and only need watching. Early detection decides whether a brace can prevent surgery.

What Is Scoliosis?

Scoliosis is a sideways curve of the spine of more than 10° on an X-ray, usually with some rotation of the vertebrae. About 80% of cases are adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS) — “idiopathic” meaning the cause is unknown — which appears between 10 and 18 years and is more common, and more likely to progress, in girls. Other types include congenital (born with abnormal vertebrae), neuromuscular (cerebral palsy, muscular dystrophy) and syndromic. Scoliosis is not caused by heavy school bags, poor posture or exercise.

Signs Parents Can Look For

  • One shoulder or shoulder blade higher or more prominent
  • Uneven waistline or one hip higher
  • Head not centred over the pelvis, or clothes that hang unevenly
  • Adams forward-bend test: when the child bends forward with arms hanging, a rib or lumbar hump on one side is the classic sign

Scoliosis is usually painless. Significant back pain, night pain, leg weakness or a stiff, rapidly progressing curve calls for further tests, because it can signal another cause.

Assessment

A standing full-spine X-ray measures the Cobb angle and shows the curve pattern. The doctor also estimates remaining growth (Risser grade on the pelvis, menarche in girls, height velocity) because the curve is most likely to progress during the growth spurt. MRI is added for atypical features (early onset, left-sided thoracic curve, neurological signs, severe pain).

Treatment by Curve Size

Less than 20°
  • Observation with X-ray every 4–6 months during growth
  • Exercise for general fitness; physiotherapy-based scoliosis-specific exercises (Schroth) may help posture and symptoms
  • Most never need more
20–45° in a growing child
  • Bracing (usually 16–23 hours a day) can stop progression in most children, lowering the need for surgery
  • Success depends on wearing it as prescribed until growth is complete
  • Brace does not correct the curve; it stops it from worsening
More than about 45–50°
  • Curves above this tend to keep progressing even after growth
  • Spinal fusion with rods and screws straightens and stabilises the spine
  • Modern surgery corrects roughly 60–80% of the curve and allows return to normal activities, though the fused segment is stiff

Living With Scoliosis

  • Children can and should stay active; sports are safe, including swimming and running
  • Encourage brace compliance by involving the child and arranging clothing and school support
  • Counsel teenagers about body image; peer support groups help
  • Regular follow-up until growth stops, and later review for adults with larger curves (back pain and curve progression can occur in adulthood)

See a Doctor Promptly If

  • A visible curve or rib hump is noticed
  • Back pain that wakes the child at night or does not settle
  • Leg weakness, numbness, or bladder/bowel changes
  • Rapid progression between visits

Common Questions

Can exercises cure scoliosis?

Exercises improve strength and posture and may help symptoms, but only bracing (for growing children) or surgery can change or stop a significant curve.

Will my daughter be able to have children?

Yes. Scoliosis and fusion surgery do not prevent pregnancy or normal delivery.

Do school screenings matter?

Yes — early detection allows bracing in time.

Is surgery dangerous?

It is a major operation done by specialists with spinal-cord monitoring; serious complications are uncommon.
