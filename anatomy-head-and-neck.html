Head & Neck Anatomy

Bones, muscles, ligaments, vessels and nerves — with origins, insertions, nerve supply, actions and the clinical points that matter, linked to a 3D model you can rotate.

Dr. Maninder SinghMS Orthopedics | Associate Professor, GMCH Amritsar
November 2026 6 min read

The neck is a crowded corridor: the cervical spine, the brain’s blood supply in the carotid and vertebral arteries, the airway, the great veins and the lymph nodes all pass through it. Muscle triangles — anterior and posterior — organise the anatomy for surgeons, and the sternocleidomastoid divides them. Knowing the vertebral artery’s course through the transverse foramina is critical in cervical spine injuries and surgery.

Bones

Skull (Cranium & Mandible)

Cranium et Mandibula · Bone · Axial Skeleton

The skull comprises 22 bones — 8 cranial bones enclosing and protecting the brain, and 14 facial bones. All are united by immobile fibrous sutures except the mandible, which articulates at the temporomandibular joint, the only truly mobile joint of the skull. The cranial base is perforated by foramina transmitting the cranial nerves and the vessels supplying the brain.

Articulations

  • Atlanto-occipital joint (with C1) — nodding "yes"
  • Temporomandibular joint (mandible) — the only mobile skull joint

Muscle & ligament attachments

  • Temporalis — temporal fossa
  • Masseter — zygomatic arch to mandibular angle
  • Sternocleidomastoid — mastoid process
  • Trapezius — superior nuchal line

Key landmarks

  • Vertex — The highest point of the skull in the anatomical position — the reference point for measuring standing height.
  • Glabella — Smooth prominence between the eyebrows above the nasal root; a standard craniometric landmark.
  • External occipital protuberance — Midline bump on the occiput (the "inion"); attachment for ligamentum nuchae and trapezius.
  • Mastoid process — Bony prominence behind the ear — insertion of sternocleidomastoid; pneumatised, so middle-ear infection can spread into it (mastoiditis).
  • Zygomatic arch — The cheekbone bridge; a common site of facial fracture and the origin of masseter.
  • Mandibular angle — Junction of body and ramus of the mandible — the second most fractured mandibular site.
  • Temporomandibular joint — Synovial joint with an intra-articular disc, allowing both hinge and gliding movement for chewing and speech.
Head injury assessment. Any skull fracture implies significant force. Basilar skull fracture signs — "raccoon eyes", Battle sign, CSF rhinorrhoea/otorrhoea — mandate urgent CT and neurosurgical review.
Cervical spine caution. In trauma, a head injury is a cervical spine injury until proven otherwise: immobilise and image before clearing the neck.

Muscles

Sternocleidomastoid (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Musculus sternocleidomastoideus · Muscle · Musculoskeletal

The prominent oblique strap of the neck with two heads (sternal and clavicular), dividing the neck into anterior and posterior triangles — the fundamental surgical map of the neck.

Origin
Manubrium sterni and medial third of the clavicle
Insertion
Mastoid process and superior nuchal line
Nerve
Spinal accessory nerve (CN XI); sensory from C2–C3
Action
Unilaterally — tilts head to same side, rotates face to opposite side; bilaterally — flexes the neck
Blood supply
Occipital and posterior auricular arteries above; superior thyroid and suprascapular below.
Venous drainage
To the external jugular and facial veins.
Lymphatic drainage
Deep cervical nodes.
Clinical relevance. Congenital muscular torticollis presents as a firm lump in the muscle in an infant with a persistently tilted head; most resolve with stretching if started early. In adults, the muscle guides the anterior approach to the cervical spine.

Scalene Muscles (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Musculi scaleni · Muscle · Musculoskeletal

Anterior, middle and posterior scalenes descend from the cervical transverse processes to the first and second ribs. The gap between anterior and middle scalene — the interscalene triangle — transmits the brachial plexus and subclavian artery.

Origin
Transverse processes of C2–C7
Insertion
First rib (anterior and middle) and second rib (posterior)
Nerve
Ventral rami of the cervical spinal nerves (C3–C8)
Action
Elevate the upper ribs in inspiration; lateral flexion of the neck
Blood supply
Ascending cervical (from the inferior thyroid) and branches of the subclavian artery.
Venous drainage
To the vertebral and subclavian veins.
Lymphatic drainage
Inferior deep cervical and supraclavicular nodes.
Clinical relevance. The interscalene triangle is the classic site of neurogenic thoracic outlet syndrome and the target for interscalene brachial plexus block. Note the subclavian *vein* passes anterior to scalenus anterior, outside the triangle — which is why venous and neurogenic outlet syndromes present so differently.

Infrahyoid (Strap) Muscles (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Musculi infrahyoidei · Muscle · Musculoskeletal

Four flat strap muscles below the hyoid — sternohyoid, sternothyroid, thyrohyoid and omohyoid — that depress the hyoid and larynx during swallowing and speech.

Origin
Manubrium, clavicle and scapula (omohyoid)
Insertion
Hyoid bone and thyroid cartilage
Nerve
Ansa cervicalis (C1–C3); thyrohyoid by C1 via the hypoglossal nerve
Action
Depress the hyoid and larynx after swallowing; stabilise the hyoid for suprahyoid action
Blood supply
Superior and inferior thyroid arteries.
Venous drainage
To the thyroid veins and internal jugular.
Lymphatic drainage
Infrahyoid, prelaryngeal and deep cervical nodes.
Clinical relevance. They are split in the midline and retracted laterally in the anterior approach to the cervical spine — the internervous plane that makes that exposure so clean. The omohyoid crosses the field at about C6 and is often divided.

Arteries

Common Carotid Artery (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Arteria carotis communis · Artery · Cardiovascular

The main arterial supply to the head and neck, ascending in the carotid sheath alongside the internal jugular vein and vagus nerve, and dividing at the upper border of the thyroid cartilage (C4) into internal and external carotid arteries.

Origin
Right — brachiocephalic trunk; left — directly from the aortic arch
Course
Ascends within the carotid sheath with the internal jugular vein and vagus nerve
Termination
Divides at C4 into internal and external carotid arteries
Surface
Pulse palpable anterior to sternocleidomastoid at the level of the thyroid cartilage
Clinical relevance. The carotid pulse is the primary check for circulation in adult resuscitation. Atherosclerotic plaque at the bifurcation is a leading cause of embolic stroke, and blunt cervical injury can cause carotid dissection — consider it in neck trauma with delayed neurological signs.

External Carotid Artery (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Arteria carotis externa · Artery · Cardiovascular

One of the two terminal branches of the common carotid, supplying the face, scalp, jaws and neck through eight named branches before ending as the superficial temporal and maxillary arteries.

Origin
Common carotid bifurcation at the upper border of the thyroid cartilage (C4)
Branches
Superior thyroid, ascending pharyngeal, lingual, facial, occipital, posterior auricular, maxillary, superficial temporal
Supplies
Face, scalp, tongue, jaws, neck musculature
Distinguishing feature
Unlike the internal carotid, it gives branches in the neck
Clinical relevance. Scalp and facial wounds bleed profusely because of its rich anastomoses across the midline — but that same redundancy means healing is excellent. The superficial temporal pulse is palpable in front of the ear and is biopsied in suspected giant cell arteritis.

Vertebral Artery (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Arteria vertebralis · Artery · Cardiovascular

Arises from the subclavian artery and ascends through the transverse foramina of C6 up to C1, then loops over the atlas to enter the skull through the foramen magnum. The two vertebral arteries join to form the basilar artery, supplying the brainstem and cerebellum.

Origin
First part of the subclavian artery
Course
Transverse foramina of C6–C1, then over the posterior arch of the atlas into the foramen magnum
Termination
Joins its fellow to form the basilar artery
Supplies
Brainstem, cerebellum, posterior cerebral circulation, cervical cord
Clinical relevance. Because it is locked inside the transverse foramina, cervical fracture-dislocations and forceful manipulation can cause vertebral artery dissection — presenting with neck pain, vertigo and posterior circulation stroke. CT angiography is indicated in displaced cervical injuries involving the foramen transversarium.

Veins

Internal Jugular Vein (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Vena jugularis interna · Vein · Cardiovascular

The principal venous drainage of the brain, face and neck. It begins at the jugular foramen as a continuation of the sigmoid sinus and runs within the carotid sheath, lateral to the common carotid artery.

Origin
Continuation of the sigmoid sinus at the jugular foramen
Course
Within the carotid sheath, lateral to the carotid artery and vagus nerve
Termination
Joins the subclavian vein to form the brachiocephalic vein
Surface
Runs between the two heads of sternocleidomastoid
Clinical relevance. The standard site for central venous catheterisation — the right side is preferred as it offers a straighter path to the heart. Jugular venous pressure, assessed at 45°, is a bedside index of right heart filling and fluid status.

External Jugular Vein (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Vena jugularis externa · Vein · Cardiovascular

A superficial vein crossing sternocleidomastoid obliquely from the angle of the jaw to the middle of the clavicle, where it pierces the deep fascia to join the subclavian vein.

Formation
Posterior auricular and posterior division of the retromandibular vein
Course
Superficial to sternocleidomastoid, within the superficial fascia
Termination
Subclavian vein
Surface
Visible from the angle of the mandible to the mid-clavicle
Clinical relevance. Its visible distension is a bedside sign of raised central venous pressure or venous obstruction. Because it lies superficially and is tethered to the deep fascia, a laceration here can admit air and cause a venous air embolism.

Lymphatics

Cervical Lymph Nodes (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Nodi lymphoidei cervicales · Lymphatics · Immune System

A deep chain along the internal jugular vein plus superficial groups, draining the head, neck and — via the thoracic duct territory — more distant sites. They are divided into six surgical levels used universally in head and neck oncology.

Levels
I submental/submandibular · II upper jugular · III mid jugular · IV lower jugular · V posterior triangle · VI anterior compartment
Afferents
Scalp, face, oral cavity, pharynx, larynx, thyroid
Efferents
Jugular lymph trunk → thoracic duct or right lymphatic duct
Relations
Deep chain lies along the internal jugular vein within the carotid sheath
Clinical relevance. Virchow's node — an enlarged left supraclavicular node (level IV) — classically signals gastric or other intra-abdominal malignancy, because the thoracic duct drains here. Persistent, hard, fixed cervical nodes in an adult require investigation for malignancy, not a course of antibiotics.

Supraclavicular Nodes (shown for one side; the other side mirrors it)

Nodi lymphoidei supraclaviculares · Lymphatics · Immune System

The lowest group of the deep cervical chain, sitting in the supraclavicular fossa at the root of the neck — level IV/V in the head and neck classification.

Location
Supraclavicular fossa, behind the clavicular head of sternocleidomastoid
Afferents
Head and neck, and — via the thoracic and right lymphatic ducts — the thorax and abdomen
Efferents
Jugular trunk → thoracic duct (left) or right lymphatic duct
Examination
Palpated from behind with the patient seated, asking them to shrug
Clinical relevance. An enlarged left supraclavicular node is Virchow node (Troisier sign) — classically gastric, but also pancreatic, ovarian or testicular malignancy, because the thoracic duct drains there. A right-sided node instead points to thoracic or oesophageal disease. Either finding in an adult demands investigation, not antibiotics.
