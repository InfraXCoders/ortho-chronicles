Hip Fracture in Older Adults:
Surgery, Recovery & Prevention

A hip fracture changes life for the patient and family. Early surgery, early walking and treating osteoporosis make the difference.

Why Hip Fractures Matter

Most hip fractures happen to people over 65 after a simple fall from standing height, because the bone has been weakened by osteoporosis. In India, hip fractures are rising with an ageing population and low vitamin D levels. The injury is serious: without prompt treatment, a person lying in bed for weeks risks pneumonia, pressure sores, blood clots and loss of independence. In well-run programmes, surgery within 24–48 hours and getting up the day after surgery reduce complications and deaths.

Types of Hip Fracture

Femoral neck fracture (inside the joint capsule)
  • Blood supply to the femoral head may be damaged
  • Undisplaced: fixed with screws
  • Displaced in older patients: hemiarthroplasty (replacing the ball) or total hip replacement
  • Younger patients: try to preserve the head with urgent fixation
Intertrochanteric fracture (just outside the capsule)
  • Good blood supply, so healing is reliable
  • Fixed with a sliding hip screw or a cephalomedullary (intramedullary) nail
  • Allows early weight-bearing in most patients

Signs of a Broken Hip

  • Severe pain in the groin or outer hip after a fall
  • Inability to stand or bear weight (although some impacted fractures can walk painfully)
  • Leg looks shortened and turned outward
  • Bruising and swelling over the hip
Do not try to stand the person up. Keep them still, support the leg, call an ambulance and avoid giving food or drink in case surgery is needed within hours.

Treatment: Almost Always Surgery

Surgery is recommended for nearly all hip fractures — even in frail patients — because it relieves pain and allows sitting and walking. A joint assessment before surgery covers heart, kidney, blood thinners, diabetes and anaemia; spinal or general anaesthesia can be used, and a nerve block gives excellent pain relief. A small group of very frail patients with minimal pre-injury function may be managed without surgery with pain control and early sitting, after a frank conversation with the family.

Recovery

1

Day 1

Out of bed to a chair, with a physiotherapist. Eating, breathing exercises and bladder care.

2

Weeks 1–6

Walking with a frame or sticks; wound check; clot prevention medicines; nutrition and protein supplements.

3

3–6 months

Improving strength and walking; most people regain walking ability, but about half need a walking aid and a significant number do not return to previous independence.

One in three older patients do not survive the first year without good care, which is why a team approach (orthopaedic surgeon, physician, physiotherapist, dietitian, family) matters.

Preventing the Next Fracture

  • Treat osteoporosis: calcium, vitamin D and, when advised, bone-strengthening medicines such as bisphosphonates
  • Check vision, medicines that cause dizziness and blood pressure on standing
  • Make the home safe: remove loose rugs, add night lights, grab bars and non-slip bathroom mats
  • Balance and strength exercises (tai chi, supervised walking, sit-to-stand)
  • Wear well-fitting, non-slip footwear

Warning Signs After Surgery

  • Fever, wound redness or discharge
  • Calf pain or swelling, or sudden breathlessness (clot)
  • Sudden new hip pain or the leg looking shorter or rotated (possible dislocation or fixation failure)
  • Confusion or drowsiness that is new (infection, medicines, low sodium)

Common Questions

Can an elderly person walk after hip fracture surgery?

Most do, usually with an aid. Starting to stand the day after surgery gives the best results.

Is surgery safe at 85 or 90?

It is safer than not operating in nearly all cases. Risks are managed with careful pre-operative preparation.

What if the family delays?

Each day of delay increases complications; seek treatment immediately.

Will it happen again?

The risk of a second fracture is high unless osteoporosis is treated.
