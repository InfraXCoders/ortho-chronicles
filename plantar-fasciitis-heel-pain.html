Plantar Fasciitis:
Why Your Heel Hurts

Those first painful steps out of bed are the signature of plantar fasciitis. The good news: over 90% of people improve without surgery.

What Is Plantar Fasciitis?

The plantar fascia is a thick band of tissue running from the heel bone to the base of the toes. It acts like a bowstring, supporting the arch and absorbing shock with every step. Repeated strain causes small tears and degeneration where the band attaches to the heel, producing pain under the heel that is worst with the first steps in the morning or after sitting for a while, eases as you warm up, and returns after long standing.

It is one of the commonest causes of heel pain in adults, affecting roughly 1 in 10 people at some time. Risk factors include being overweight, standing for long hours on hard floors, sudden increases in walking or running, tight calf muscles, flat or very high arches, and unsupportive footwear such as thin chappals or worn-out shoes.

Diagnosis

A doctor can usually diagnose plantar fasciitis from the history and by pressing on the inner part of the heel. An X-ray is not required for the diagnosis, but it is sometimes done to look for a stress fracture or arthritis. A heel spur seen on X-ray is common in people with no pain at all and is not the cause of the pain; treating the spur does not treat the pain. Other causes of heel pain — fat-pad atrophy, nerve entrapment, Achilles problems, stress fracture, and in younger people inflammatory arthritis — are considered when the picture is atypical or treatment fails.

What Actually Works

1

Calf and plantar-fascia stretching

Before getting out of bed, pull the toes toward the shin for 30 seconds and repeat 3 times; stretch the calf against a wall twice a day. Stretching is the best-evidenced treatment.

2

Footwear and arch support

Wear cushioned shoes with a firm arch; avoid walking barefoot on hard floors. Over-the-counter heel cups or a soft arch insert often help; custom orthoses are only occasionally needed.

3

Load management & weight

Reduce high-impact activities for a few weeks, swap running for cycling or swimming, and lose weight if overweight — every kilogram less eases force through the heel.

4

Short-term pain relief

Ice (roll the foot over a frozen water bottle), and a short course of anti-inflammatory tablets or gel as advised by your doctor.

5

Night splint

A splint that holds the foot gently flexed overnight keeps the fascia stretched and eases the first-step pain.

Most people are much better in 6–12 weeks; complete resolution can take up to a year, so patience and consistency matter.

If It Is Not Settling

When 3–6 months of regular conservative treatment has not worked, options include:

  • Extracorporeal shockwave therapy (ESWT) — painless sound-wave sessions that stimulate healing; evidence is moderate and generally safe
  • Corticosteroid injection — can provide short-term relief but repeated injections risk weakening or rupturing the fascia and thinning the heel fat pad, so they are used sparingly
  • Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) — may help some patients; costs vary and evidence is still developing
  • Surgery (partial plantar fascia release) — needed in fewer than 5–10% of patients with long-standing, disabling pain, after other causes are excluded

Preventing a Relapse

  • Continue calf stretches even after pain settles
  • Replace worn shoes every 6–12 months of heavy use
  • Increase walking or running distance gradually (no more than about 10% a week)
  • Alternate hard-floor standing with cushioned mats if your work needs long standing
  • Maintain a healthy weight

See a Doctor If

  • Heel pain after an injury or a sudden “pop”
  • Redness, warmth, fever or swelling of the heel
  • Numbness, burning or tingling in the foot, which may suggest a nerve problem
  • Pain at night at rest, or in both heels with stiff joints elsewhere
  • No improvement after 6–8 weeks of proper stretching and footwear changes

Common Questions

Is a heel spur the cause of my pain?

Usually not. Spurs are seen in many pain-free people. The pain comes from the strained fascia where it attaches to the bone.

Should I keep walking?

Yes, but in cushioned footwear and in short, regular periods. Avoid long barefoot walks and high-impact sports until the pain eases.

How long will it last?

Most people recover within a few months; about 1 in 10 need a year or more.

Are expensive custom insoles needed?

Not usually. Many people do as well with cheap off-the-shelf insoles and good stretching.
