ACL Injury:
Surgery & Rehabilitation

A pop, a swollen knee and a feeling of instability: the anterior cruciate ligament is the most commonly reconstructed ligament in sport. This is the full pathway from injury to return to play.

What Is the ACL and How Does It Tear?

The anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) is a strong band inside the knee that stops the shin bone sliding forward and controls rotation. It usually tears in a non-contact twisting or landing injury — changing direction on a planted foot, landing awkwardly from a jump — in football, cricket, badminton, kabaddi, basketball, skiing and similar sports. Many people hear or feel a “pop”, the knee swells within hours (a sign of bleeding into the joint), and it feels unstable or “gives way”.

Women have a higher risk than men in the same sport, partly because of differences in alignment, muscle activation patterns and landing mechanics. The ACL commonly tears together with the meniscus or medial collateral ligament, which influences treatment.

Diagnosis

Examination with the Lachman test, anterior drawer and pivot-shift tests is highly accurate in the hands of an experienced surgeon. An X-ray is taken to exclude fractures (including a Segond fracture, a clue to ACL injury), and MRI confirms the tear and shows meniscus, cartilage and other ligament damage — which is crucial for planning.

First Aid and Early Care

  • RICE: rest, ice for 15–20 minutes every few hours, a compression bandage and elevation
  • Use crutches until you can walk without a limp
  • Start gentle movement within days: straightening the knee fully and regaining bend
  • Begin physiotherapy early — a stiff knee at the time of surgery gives worse results, so “prehabilitation” (reducing swelling, restoring full extension and quadriceps strength) is part of preparation
  • Surgery is not usually done in the first weeks — it is planned once swelling has settled

Do I Need Surgery?

Not everyone does. The decision depends on your age, activity goals, the knee’s stability and other injuries:

Rehabilitation alone may suit
  • Low-demand, mostly straight-line activities
  • Older patients or those with other health problems
  • People with no giving-way episodes after structured rehab
  • Some skeletally immature children in selected cases, with specialist advice
Reconstruction is recommended for
  • Pivoting or contact sports (football, badminton, kabaddi)
  • Recurrent giving-way in daily life
  • Associated repairable meniscus tear or other ligament injury
  • Young active patients and people with physical-labour jobs

Unrepaired ACL-deficient knees that keep giving way risk further meniscal and cartilage damage and earlier arthritis, which is the strongest argument for operating in active people.

ACL Reconstruction: How It Is Done

The torn ligament is not stitched back — it is replaced with a graft through keyhole (arthroscopic) surgery under spinal or general anaesthetic, taking about 60–90 minutes. Graft options include the hamstring tendons (the most common in India), the patellar tendon, the quadriceps tendon and, in some cases, donor tissue. Tunnels are drilled in the thigh and shin bones and the graft is fixed with screws or buttons. Associated meniscus tears are repaired or trimmed at the same sitting. Most patients go home the same day or the next.

Rehabilitation Timeline

1

0–6 weeks — protect and move

Control swelling, regain full knee straightening, bend to 90–120°, walk normally with or without crutches, activate the quadriceps.

2

6–12 weeks — build strength

Cycling, closed-chain strengthening (squats, leg press), balance training, normal walking pattern, no running yet.

3

3–6 months — jog and control

Straight-line jogging after clearance, plyometric basics, hop tests, hamstring and hip strength.

4

6–9+ months — return to sport

Sport-specific drills, cutting and pivoting, neuromuscular control. Return only after passing objective tests.

Returning too early is the main cause of re-tear. Recent studies suggest a minimum of 9 months, with the risk of re-injury falling for each additional month up to about 9–12 months, and strong evidence that passing strength (limb symmetry above ~90%) and hop tests lowers the re-rupture rate.

Call Your Surgeon If

  • Fever, redness or discharge from the wounds
  • Calf swelling or pain (possible clot) or sudden breathlessness
  • Increasing pain or swelling after a period of improvement
  • Inability to straighten the knee fully after 4–6 weeks (loss of extension)

Common Questions

Can an ACL tear heal by itself?

A completely torn ACL does not heal on its own because the ends retract in the joint fluid, but some people function well without it after rehab.

How long off work?

Desk work: about 1–2 weeks. Manual or standing work: 3–6 months, depending on demands.

Is ACL surgery safe?

It is a well-established operation. Risks include stiffness, infection, graft failure and rarely blood clot or nerve irritation.

Will I get arthritis anyway?

Risk is higher after ACL injury irrespective of surgery, especially with associated meniscal damage; reconstruction and good rehab help protect the knee.
